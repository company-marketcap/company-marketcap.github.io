        What Is My Net Worth? The Formula Explained

        Your net worth is a snapshot of your financial position on a single day. Some people type "networth" as one word when they search for it, but the idea is the same: it is the market value of everything you hold, minus every balance you still owe. It is a measure of wealth, not of income. A high salary can sit next to a thin balance sheet, and a modest salary can build real wealth when the gap between what comes in and what goes out is invested. If you want to see how the figures change, the benefit of spending less calculator gives you an instant result you can adjust as you go.

        The calculation is a single subtraction:

        $$\text{Net worth} = \text{Total assets} - \text{Total liabilities}$$

        When the result is above zero you have a positive net worth. When your liabilities are larger than your assets, you have a negative net worth, which is common for recent graduates and for anyone who has just bought a house with a small down payment.

        What You Own: Total Assets

        Assets are items you could sell or draw on for cash. Add up the total assets across every category below, using the current market value rather than what you paid:

        • Cash in a checking or savings account
        • Retirement accounts such as 401(k)s or an IRA, plus any pension value
        • Brokerage and other investment accounts holding stocks, bonds and mutual funds
        • Real estate and land, including the house you live in
        • Vehicles, boats, art, collections, gold and other valuables
        • Insurance policies that carry a cash value, and fixed deposits

        What You Owe: Total Liabilities

        Liabilities are debts you are responsible for repaying. Your total liabilities include:

        • Your mortgage or home loan
        • Car loans and student loans
        • Credit card balances and any other credit card debt
        • Medical debt, business loans and any personal loan
        • Taxes due and other unpaid bills

        Income, such as your paycheck, is not on either list. It feeds your balance sheet over time, but it is not part of the snapshot itself.

        How to Use a Net Worth Calculator Step by Step

        Gather your latest statements so every figure reflects a single date, then work through the steps in order: The should my spouse work calculator is free to use with no sign-up, and works on desktop and mobile.

        1. Write down the current balance of each savings account and checking account.
        2. Add retirement and investment accounts, using today's balance.
        3. Estimate the market value of your home and car, using a recent comparable sale or a listing guide.
        4. Record the outstanding balance on every loan and card.
        5. Subtract the liability total from the asset total.

        A calculator saves the arithmetic, but the quality of the answer still depends on how honest the inputs are. Round nothing up, and do not count items you could not realistically sell.

        Net Worth Calculator Tips for Accurate Results

        Use one date
        Pull every balance from the same day so the snapshot is consistent.
        Use realistic value
        Enter what an item would sell for today, not its purchase price or sentimental worth.
        Track the balance every quarter
        A single reading tells you where you stand; a series of readings shows whether spending and saving are working.

        Treat the final figure as a starting point for your own plan, not a verdict on how well you are doing.

        Plugging the totals into the formula gives \(438{,}635 - 232{,}845 = 205{,}790\), so this household's net worth is $205,790. The house alone contributes $88,080 of that (its $286,500 market value less the $198,420 mortgage), and the car adds only $4,330 once its loan is subtracted.

        Reading Your Debt to Asset Ratio

        The debt to asset ratio shows how much of what you hold is financed by borrowing:

        $$\text{Debt to asset ratio} = \frac{\text{Total liabilities}}{\text{Total assets}} = \frac{232{,}845}{438{,}635} \approx 0.53$$

        A ratio of 53% means more than half of the household's holdings are still owed to lenders. Paying down $10,000 of debt would lower the ratio to roughly 51% and lift the total to $215,790. The ratio falls as liabilities shrink or assets grow, and it is a useful risk check alongside the bottom-line figure.

        Liquid Assets, Partial Liquid Assets and Illiquid Assets

        Not every dollar of net worth is equally easy to spend. Liquid assets can be turned into cash within days, partial liquid assets like retirement balances carry penalties or delays, and illiquid assets such as a house take months to sell. Some tools show each class as a percentage of net worth; here the clearer comparison is the share of total assets:

        ClassValueShare of total assets
        Liquid assets$41,1209.4%
        Partial liquid assets$96,81522.1%
        Illiquid assets$300,70068.6%

        With 68.6% of its assets locked in a house and a car, this household would feel cash-tight in an emergency even though its total looks healthy.

        Finding My Net Worth Before a Mortgage Pre-Approval

        Dani, 29, is about to meet a loan officer and wants to know what the lender will see. The statements are open: $4,912.38 in checking, $31,406.75 in savings, $18,774.20 in a Roth IRA, $9,640.15 in a 401(k), and a used hatchback worth about $11,250 on a pricing guide. That is $75,983.48 of assets.

        On the debt side Dani enters $38,615.40 of student loans, a $12,204.66 car loan and $1,987.31 on a credit card, which makes $52,807.37 of liabilities. The net worth figure comes back as $23,176.11, with a debt to asset ratio of 69.5%.

        Dani compares that against the Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances, where the median household under 35 holds roughly $39,040. At $23,176.11 Dani sits $15,863.89 below that median, which explains why the student loans, not the card, dominate the picture.

        The next question is the down payment. Dani reruns the form with savings cut to $17,406.75 after setting aside $14,000 for the purchase. Net worth stays at $23,176.11, because cash simply becomes home equity, but liquid assets drop to $22,319.13. Against $5,400 of monthly expenses that is 4.1 months of cover, still above the three-month reserve most lenders like to see. Dani keeps the $14,000 plan and books the appointment.

        Positive Net Worth vs Negative Net Worth

        A positive result means you own more than you owe, and a negative result means debt outweighs what you hold. Neither is a permanent label. A negative net worth is common early in a career, and what matters is the direction over time. Appreciating assets, such as investments that grow or real estate in a rising market, push the figure up, while depreciating assets like vehicles pull it down.

        Retirement Accounts and Financial Health in Your Net Worth

        Your retirement accounts often make up the largest slice of an adult's wealth outside of housing. Every balance counts on the asset side of your total, and because those balances are invested in fixed income and equities, they swing with markets, so re-enter them each time you update your financial health picture. A retirement planner can tell you whether the trajectory is on pace; the balance sheet tells you where you stand today.

        Why Your Income Is Not Your Net Worth

        Two people earning the same salary can end up with very different figures. The person who budgets, tracks spending and invests the difference builds assets, while the person who uses credit to cover the gap adds liabilities. Take a household earning $500,000 yet spending more than it makes: its net worth can still be negative.

        Ways to Grow Your Net Worth

        Once you know the number, you can act on it. The two levers are raising assets and cutting liabilities, and both show up directly in the result when you rerun the form, and both are best handled through budgeting and steady saving.

        Debt Paydown and Your Emergency Fund

        Tackle high-interest balances first, because a credit card charging a double-digit rate erodes your net worth faster than a mortgage does. Pair debt paydown with an emergency fund so a surprise bill does not force you back into borrowing.

        Investing, Cash Flow and Long-Term Goals

        Positive cash flow is the engine behind investing. Direct the surplus into a diversified portfolio, which raises the asset side of your net worth, weigh the risk you can tolerate, and tie each deposit to a goal such as financial independence. If your balance sheet is complex, a financial advisor can help you plan, and re-entering your figures every quarter shows how each deposit moved the result.

        Net Worth Calculator questions

        What is net worth?

        Net worth is the value of everything you own (assets) minus everything you owe (liabilities). It is a snapshot of your finances on one day, not a measure of income.

        How is net worth calculated?

        Add up your total assets, such as cash, investments, retirement accounts, property and vehicles, then subtract your total liabilities, such as a mortgage, car loans, student loans and credit card balances.

        Can my net worth be negative?

        Yes. If you owe more than you own, your net worth is negative. That is common early in a career or soon after taking out a large loan, and it can change as you pay down debt and build assets.

        Is my income part of my net worth?

        No. Net worth measures wealth you already hold. Income only affects it over time, when you save or invest what you earn instead of spending it.

        What counts as a liquid, partial liquid or illiquid asset?

        Liquid assets, like savings, can be turned into cash quickly. Partial liquid assets, like retirement or deposit accounts, can be accessed but may carry delays or penalties. Illiquid assets, like property, take time to sell.

        What is the debt to asset ratio?

        It is your total liabilities divided by your total assets. A lower ratio means less of what you own is financed by borrowing.

        Should I count my home as an asset?

        Yes, at its current market value, as long as you also include the mortgage balance as a liability. Only the difference between the two adds to your net worth.

        How often should I recalculate my net worth?

        Many people update it quarterly or once a year, using balances from the same date, so they can see whether saving and debt paydown are moving the number.
