        How a Balloon Loan Works

        A balloon loan is a short term loan whose monthly payments are calculated as if the debt would be repaid over a much longer period. Because the payment schedule stretches over, say, 20 or 30 years while the loan itself lasts only 5 or 7, the loan does not fully amortize, and most of the principal is still outstanding when the term ends. That unpaid amount is the balloon payment, and it is due in one piece. If you want to see how the figures change, the option arm calculator online gives you an instant result you can adjust as you go.

        Lenders offer this structure because it keeps each installment affordable while limiting how long they carry the risk. Borrowers usually accept it because they expect to refinance, sell, or receive money before the final due date. Many borrowers who choose a balloon mortgage do so for exactly that reason. The balloon mortgage is the best-known version, but the same design appears in car loans, bridge loans, and commercial real estate financing.

        Loan Term vs. Amortization Period

        Two different clocks run on every balloon loan. The loan term (also called the balloon period) is how long the loan actually lasts. The amortization period is the number of years used to calculate the monthly payment. A 5-year term with a 25-year schedule means you make 60 payments sized for a 300-payment loan, then owe whatever balance is left. This spread is called the loan amortization period in many lender disclosures.

        • Term: the date the whole remaining balance comes due.
        • Amortization: the schedule used to size each installment; a longer one lowers it but raises the balloon.
        • Rate: the yearly figure, divided by 12 for each monthly accrual.

        Balloon Payment Calculator Formula

        The monthly payment comes from the standard amortization formula, applied to the full schedule rather than the shorter term: Pair this with the apr calculator for adjustable rate mortgages for a fuller picture before you make a decision.

        $$M = P \times \frac{i\,(1+i)^{n}}{(1+i)^{n}-1}$$

        Here M is the installment, P is the loan amount, i is the monthly rate (annual rate ÷ 12) and n is the number of payments in the schedule. The balloon payment is the remaining balance after k payments, where k is the number of months in the loan term:

        $$B = P\,(1+i)^{k} - M \times \frac{(1+i)^{k}-1}{i}$$

        Total interest cost over the term equals the sum of all installments plus the balloon, minus the original loan amount: \(\text{Interest} = (M \times k) + B - P\).

        Interest-Only Payments

        Some lenders, especially on commercial mortgage deals, allow interest-only payments during the term. The installment is simply \(P \times i\), no principal is repaid, and the balloon equals the full loan amount. It is the lowest possible outlay, and also the largest balloon.

        How Amortization Changes the Balloon Mortgage

        The length of the schedule is the lever with the biggest effect on a balloon mortgage. Using the same $265,000 loan at 6.75% and a 5-year term, the table compares four lengths, including the 30-year amortization that is typical of a fixed-rate mortgage.

        Schedule lengthMonthly paymentBalloon paymentTotal interest
        15 years$2,345.01$204,226.27$79,926.88
        20 years$2,014.96$227,702.91$83,600.79
        25 years$1,830.92$240,794.61$85,649.54
        30 years$1,718.78$248,770.63$86,897.73

        Moving from a 15-year to a 30-year schedule cuts the installment by $626.23 a month, but the large payment due at the end grows by $44,544.36. That trade-off is the core of every balloon decision: you borrow breathing room in the monthly budget and pay for it with a bigger bill later.

        Interest Rate Sensitivity

        The rate matters less to the installment than the schedule length does, but it still moves the balloon. On a 20-year schedule, a rate of 5.75% gives $1,861 a month and a balloon of $224,048, while 7.75% gives $2,176 and $231,124. A one-point change shifts the monthly cost by roughly $160, yet the balloon moves by only about $3,500, because most of the balance survives the short term either way. Check current mortgage rates for your credit profile before you model a scenario, since quoted rates differ widely between a bank, a credit union and a private lender.

        How to Use This Balloon Payment Calculator

        Follow these steps to model your own loan:

        1. Enter the loan amount, the original balance you expect to borrow.
        2. Enter the annual interest rate or APR quoted by the lender.
        3. Set the loan term, the number of years until the balloon is due.
        4. Set the amortization period used to size each installment.
        5. Read the result fields, then print the report or review the amortization schedule.

        Tip: Run the numbers twice, once with your lender's schedule and once with a shorter one. The gap in the balloon shows how much risk the lower payment is buying.

        Balloon Versus a 5-Year Fully Amortizing Loan

        To see what the lower installment is worth, compare the example with a conventional 5-year loan for the same $265,000 at 6.75%. The conventional version costs $5,216.12 a month, which is $3,201.16 more than the balloon version, but nothing is owed at the end and total interest falls to $47,967.02. The balloon saves roughly $3,200 every month for five years and costs about $35,634 more in interest, and it leaves you holding the unpaid balance.

        Planning for the Balloon Payment

        This type of financing is only as safe as your plan for the final payment. Lenders do not automatically convert the remaining balance into a traditional loan when the term is over, so you need one of three exits ready well before the due date.

        Refinancing the Balloon Loan

        Refinancing replaces the balance with a new, fully amortizing mortgage. On the $227,702.91 balloon from the example, a 20-year refinance at 5.75% would cost $1,598.66 a month, at 6.75% it would be $1,731.37, and at 7.75% it would be $1,869.32. Your ability to qualify depends on your credit, income, and the property's value at the time, not on what you qualified for originally.

        Selling the Property

        If you plan to sell the property before the term ends, compare the balloon payment the calculator shows with your expected net sale proceeds after closing costs. If the proceeds fall short, the sale will not clear the debt, which is why balloon financing suits short, predictable plans.

        Paying the Lump Sum in Cash

        Some borrowers save toward the lump sum payment instead. On the $227,702.91 balloon from the example, that means about $3,795 a month for 60 months in a sinking fund, set aside in your budget from day one rather than hoped for later.

        If You Cannot Make the Balloon Payment

        The balloon figure the calculator shows is the amount that triggers default if it is unpaid on the due date, and the lender may begin foreclosure or repossession. Talk to your lender at least six months before the due date about an extension or a refinance, because most are more flexible before a missed deadline than after.

        Types of Loans With a Balloon Payment

        Commercial Mortgages

        A commercial mortgage on commercial real estate, such as an office, a warehouse or a retail unit, frequently has a 5- to 10-year term with a 25- or 30-year amortization. Bridge loans and hard money loans are shorter, often 1 to 3 years, and typically interest-only, with higher rates to match their risk. Agency programs from Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac also finance multifamily mortgage loans with balloon structures, and many commercial borrowers refinance with another commercial lender when the term ends.

        Residential Mortgages

        Residential mortgages with balloons usually run 5 to 7 years and are offered by credit union and community bank lenders that hold the loan on their own books. Since Regulation Z under the Truth in Lending Act requires lenders to confirm your ability-to-repay, a balloon mortgage still needs documented income, even though the monthly payment is small.

        Car Loans

        Some auto financing uses a balloon so the monthly payments stay low, leaving a residual payment at the end that you can pay, refinance, or settle by returning or trading in the vehicle.

        Balloon Mortgage Rates and Qualifying

        Mortgage rates on a balloon structure are often a little lower than on a 30-year fixed-rate loan, since the lender is exposed for fewer years. Rates still vary by lender type: a credit union may price a residential mortgage differently than a regional bank, and private lenders charge far more on hard money loans. Compare the APR rather than the headline rate so that points and fees are included. Type the quoted rate or APR into the calculator's rate field and the balloon updates immediately, so test the quote for your exact term: the rates shown for a 7-year balloon are not the same as those for a 3-year one.

        Qualifying matters because the balloon the calculator returns is what you must refinance. A commercial mortgage underwriter looks at the property's income, while a residential mortgage lender focuses on income, credit and debt, and many lenders also test whether you could realistically refinance when the balloon comes due.

        Pros and Cons of a Balloon Mortgage

        • Lower monthly payment: sizing payments to a long schedule frees up cash flow.
        • Short-term savings: useful if you will sell or refinance soon, since you rarely benefit from paying down principal slowly.
        • Refinancing risk: rates, credit, or property values may be worse when the balloon comes due, and borrowers who cannot refinance may face a forced sale.
        • Little equity growth: principal payments are small in the early years, so the debt stays high.
        • Payment shock: the final bill can be many times your monthly payment.

        A balloon loan fits you if your income is expected to rise, your holding period is short, or you can pay the lump sum from a known source. It is a poor fit if you need predictable long-term financing and cannot absorb the refinancing risk. Before committing, compare the loan against a plain fixed-rate mortgage using the same inputs.

        Balloon Loan Calculator Questions About Costs

        Does the Calculator Include Taxes and Fees?

        No. The figures cover principal and interest only. Because the balloon payment is principal, taxes and insurance do not change it, and an origination fee can be rolled in by raising the loan amount. Add property taxes, insurance and closing costs to your own budget when you compare offers.

        Why Is My Total Interest So High Relative to Principal Repaid?

        Interest accrues on the full outstanding balance, which barely falls in the early years. That is why the $120,897.88 paid in the example repays only $37,297.09 of the loan.

        Balloon Loan Calculator questions

        What is a balloon loan?

        A balloon loan is a short-term loan whose monthly payments are calculated over a longer amortization schedule. Because the payments do not fully repay the debt, the remaining balance comes due as one large balloon payment when the term ends.

        How is a balloon payment calculated?

        Take the loan balance after the payments you have made. The calculator applies the standard amortization formula to the full schedule, then finds what is still owed at the end of the loan term. That remaining balance is the balloon payment.

        What is the difference between the loan term and the amortization period?

        The loan term is how long the loan actually lasts before the balloon is due. The amortization period is the number of years used to size each monthly payment. A longer amortization lowers the payment but increases the balloon.

        What happens if I cannot make the balloon payment?

        Balloon loans do not convert automatically into regular loans. You would need to refinance the balance, sell the asset, or pay it from savings. Missing it can lead to default, so talk to your lender well before the due date.

        Can I enter a balloon amount instead of an amortization schedule?

        Yes. Set the amortization schedule to 0 and enter the balloon amount your lender quoted. The calculator then solves for the monthly payment that leaves exactly that balance at the end of the term.

        Are balloon payments only used for mortgages?

        No. Balloon structures also appear in commercial real estate loans, bridge loans, and some car loans. Residential balloon mortgages typically run 5 to 7 years, while bridge and hard money loans are often shorter.

        How does the origination fee affect the results?

        A fee can be paid upfront or added to the loan. Adding it increases the amount financed, which raises the monthly payment, total interest and balloon. Paying it upfront shows up in the Paid Upfront result.

        Does this calculator include taxes and insurance?

        No. The results cover principal and interest, plus any origination fee you enter. Property taxes, insurance and other closing costs are not included, so add them to your budget separately.
