        How a Salary Calculator Converts Annual Salary to Every Pay Frequency

        A salary is quoted as one yearly figure, but you live on weekly, bi-weekly or monthly deposits. Converting between them is simple division, and the only thing that changes is the number of pay periods per year. Weekly pay uses 52 periods, bi-weekly pay uses 26, semi-monthly pay uses 24, monthly pay uses 12 and quarterly pay uses 4.

        $$\text{Pay per period} = \frac{\text{Annual salary}}{\text{Pay periods per year}}$$

        For the hourly rate, divide by the hours you are expected to work. A standard full-time schedule is 40 hours across 52 working weeks, which gives 2,080 hours worked per year, so the formula becomes:

        $$\text{Hourly rate} = \frac{\text{Annual salary}}{40 \times 52}$$

        Take an annual salary of $71,340. The table below shows what that single figure looks like at every pay frequency, using all 260 weekdays as paid working days.

        Pay frequencyAmount for a $71,340 salary
        Hourly$34.30
        Daily$274.38
        Weekly$1,371.92
        Bi-weekly$2,743.85
        Semi-monthly$2,972.50
        Monthly$5,945.00
        Quarterly$17,835.00
        Annual$71,340.00

        Unadjusted vs adjusted salary

        The unadjusted hourly rate in the table spreads your salary across all 260 weekdays, including the ones you do not work. The adjusted rate removes holidays and vacation days from the working days first. With 9 holidays and 11 vacation days you have 240 working days, so the same $71,340 works out to $297.25 per day and $37.16 for each hour actually worked. That gap of $2.86 per hour matters when you compare a salaried job against a contractor quote, because the contractor is not paid for the days off.

        Bi-weekly vs semi-monthly pay

        These two schedules sound alike but are not. Bi-weekly means every other week, which produces 26 paychecks, and two months each year include a third paycheck. Semi-monthly means twice a month, usually on the 15th and the last day, which produces 24 paychecks. The same $71,340 pays $2,743.85 per bi-weekly check but $2,972.50 per semi-monthly check, and the yearly total is identical.

        Gross Pay vs Net Pay: Calculating Take Home Pay from Your Salary

        Gross pay is what you earn before anything is removed. Net pay, also called net income or your take-home, is what remains after taxes, benefits and other deductions. The distance between the two is why a raise or job offer never feels as large as the headline figure. The gross-to-net sequence works in a fixed order:

        1. Start with gross pay for the pay period.
        2. Subtract pre-tax deductions such as health insurance and 401(k) contributions.
        3. Withhold federal income tax, FICA and any state or local taxes.
        4. Subtract post-tax deductions such as garnishments or Roth contributions.
        5. What is left is your net pay.

        $$\text{Net pay} = \text{Gross pay} - \text{Pre-tax deductions} - \text{Taxes} - \text{Post-tax deductions}$$

        The gross pay method: annual amount vs per period amount

        Some tools ask whether you are entering an annual amount or a per period amount. The annual amount is your full-year gross pay, while the per period amount is what a single payday pays before withholding. Entering the wrong one is the most common reason an estimate looks off, because a $2,743.85 bi-weekly figure typed into an annual field would imply a tiny yearly income.

        Federal Paycheck Calculator Logic: Federal Income Tax Withholding

        A federal paycheck calculator does not tax your gross salary directly. It starts from your yearly gross pay, removes pre-tax items and the standard deduction, applies the tax brackets to what remains and divides the result by the pay periods in the year. Your employer follows the same steps using the information on your Form W-4.

        Form W-4 and filing status

        The Form W-4 you hand your employer controls the withholding. It records your filing status, whether you hold more than one job and how many dependents or tax credits you claim. Every employee on a payroll chooses one, and a married employee who files jointly is taxed differently from a single employee with the same income. The common statuses are:

        • Single, for an unmarried worker with no qualifying dependents
        • Married filing jointly, which uses wider brackets and a larger standard deduction
        • Head of household, for an unmarried worker supporting a qualifying dependent

        Tax brackets and your marginal rate

        The United States uses progressive tax brackets, so only the income inside each band is charged that band's rate. Your marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar, not the rate on all of it. These are the first three federal brackets for 2026, applied to taxable income after the standard deduction. A married couple filing jointly uses the wider right-hand column.

        RateSingle filersMarried filing jointly
        10%Up to $12,400Up to $24,800
        12%$12,401 to $50,400$24,801 to $100,800
        22%$50,401 to $105,700$100,801 to $211,400

        Because the standard deduction is $16,100 for a single filer, a worker earning $71,340 and putting aside retirement contributions and health premiums reaches only the 12% bracket. Most of the federal tax in that case comes from the 12% band, not the 22% one a quick glance at the gross salary might suggest.

        FICA Paycheck Taxes: Social Security and Medicare

        FICA is a flat payroll tax that funds Social Security and Medicare. It does not depend on your filing status. The employee pays 6.2% for Social Security and 1.45% for Medicare, and the employer pays the same amounts on top, so the real cost of your employment is higher than your gross pay suggests.

        Wage base limit and additional Medicare tax

        Social Security applies only up to a wage base limit, which is $184,500 for 2026. Once your earnings pass it, that deduction stops for the rest of the year. Medicare has no cap, and higher earners owe the additional Medicare tax of 0.9% on wages above $200,000, a charge the employee alone pays. Both are part of your payroll taxes.

        Pre-Tax Deductions and Post-Tax Deductions That Change Your Paycheck

        Not every deduction is treated alike. Pre-tax deductions come out before income tax is calculated, so they lower your taxable income. Post-tax deductions come out afterward and give no tax break. Common examples include:

        • Pre-tax: health insurance premiums, 401(k) deferrals, flexible spending accounts and commuter plans
        • Post-tax: Roth contributions, union dues, charitable payroll giving and court-ordered wage garnishments

        A 401(k) contribution lowers federal income tax but not FICA, while a health premium paid through a cafeteria plan lowers both. That difference is built into the worked example below.

        Benefits that count as pay

        Healthcare cover, employer retirement matches, paid time off and other benefits add real value that the paycheck never displays. Total compensation includes the employer match on employer-sponsored 401(k)s and the paid holidays, but the calculator only subtracts the employee's own share of premiums and contributions, so an estimated net pay understates what the job is worth. When you compare two offers, list each employer-paid item with a yearly value and weigh it beside the base wages.

        Wage garnishments

        Wage garnishments arrive by court order, for example for unpaid taxes, child support or a defaulted loan. Your employer must withhold them, and they reduce your net pay even though they are not taxes.

        Take-Home Pay Check on a $64,280 Job Offer in Tampa

        A pharmacy technician in Tampa earns $29.15 per hour and has just received an offer at $64,280 a year. The technician is single, paid bi-weekly, pays $96.20 per check for health coverage and keeps a 4% 401(k) deferral. Florida has no state income tax, so the only question is how much more lands in the account each payday.

        First the current job: $29.15 × 2,080 hours = $60,632, or $2,332.00 gross per paycheck. The calculator returns $1,798.23 net. Next the offer: $64,280 ÷ 26 gives $2,472.31 gross, and the result reads $1,906.02, a gain of $107.79 per check, or $2,802.54 over 26 pay periods. A $3,648 raise in gross terms turns into a smaller real gain because 7.65% goes to FICA and 12% to federal income tax.

        The technician's rent and car payment need at least $1,850.00 from each check, and the new employer matches 401(k) deferrals up to 6%. So the next run changes one input: the deferral moves from 4% to 6%. Net pay falls to $1,862.51, still $12.51 above the $1,850.00 floor. Taxable income drops to $41,822.00, which sits $8,578.00 below the $50,400 ceiling of the 12% single-filer bracket, so the marginal rate stays at 12%.

        ScenarioGross per checkNet per check
        Current job, 4% deferral$2,332.00$1,798.23
        Offer, 4% deferral$2,472.31$1,906.02
        Offer, 6% deferral$2,472.31$1,862.51

        The decision follows from the numbers: accept the offer, set the deferral at 6% to capture the full match, and keep the $12.51 cushion for the first month of a new commute.

        2. Select your pay frequency, from weekly through monthly.

        4. Add your pre-tax deductions, then your post-tax deductions.
        5. Click the Calculate button and read the gross pay, withholding lines and net pay.

        Change one input at a time, such as a larger 401(k) contribution, and watch how the net pay moves. That comparison shows the true price of each decision.

        How to Calculate Annual Income from an Hourly Rate

        Going the other direction is just as common. If you are offered an hourly rate, multiply it by the hours you work each week and by 52 weeks. At $27.65 per hour for 40 hours, annual income is $27.65 × 40 × 52 = $57,512. Divide by 26 for a bi-weekly gross of $2,212.00 or by 12 for $4,792.67 per month. Remember that hourly work with unpaid holidays or unpaid vacation days produces a lower real yearly figure, so count the weeks you will actually be paid.

        Working backward from the take-home you need

        Sometimes you know the net amount you want per paycheck and need the gross salary that delivers it. Divide the desired net pay by one minus your overall withholding rate. In the worked example, $695.41 of every $2,743.85 is withheld or deferred, an overall rate of about 25.3%, so a goal of $2,500.00 net per bi-weekly check needs a gross near $3,348.71 per period, or roughly $87,066 a year. Treat that as a guide, because a higher salary can move you into a bigger bracket.

        Ways to Raise Your Take-Home Pay Without a New Salary

        If the estimate disappoints you, the annual salary is not the only lever. Each of these changes alters the tax calculation and is easy to test by re-running the numbers:

        • Raise your pre-tax retirement contributions, which lowers the income that federal income tax is charged on
        • Move health costs into a flexible spending account or health savings account, which also lowers FICA
        • Review your W-4 after marriage, a new child or a second job, so the withholding matches your real liability
        • Ask whether your employer offers a commuter plan or a remote-work stipend

        Each option trades some of today's cash for a tax saving or a future benefit, so decide which matters more to you. A larger 401(k) deferral, for instance, cuts the paycheck now but builds the account that supports you in retirement, and the immediate tax saving softens the drop.

        When withholding is too high or too low

        Withholding is an estimate, not your final bill, and these withholdings only settle when you file your return. If your employer withholds more than you owe, you receive the difference as a refund after filing, which is really an interest-free loan to the government. If it withholds too little, you owe money in April and possibly a penalty. Comparing the calculator's federal figure with the amount on your pay stub is the quickest way to spot either problem early, and a revised W-4 corrects it for the remaining pay periods.

        Overtime and bonus income complicate the comparison. The calculator spreads whatever you enter evenly across the year, so a single large bonus paycheck will differ from the estimated net pay for that period, even though the yearly tax still depends on your taxable income for the whole year rather than on any one deposit.

        Inputs that explain a drop in net pay

        Beyond taxes, three quiet changes shrink the deposit, and each maps to a field in the calculator: a mid-year jump in your 401(k) contribution, a switch to a pricier medical plan (both pre-tax deduction fields), or a raise that pushes part of your income into the next bracket. None of these signals an error. Re-enter the new values whenever a benefit election changes so the result matches the deposit you will actually see.

        Salary vs Hourly Wage: Overtime, Bonus Pay and Contractors

        A wage is tied to hours, while a salary is tied to the job. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, a non-exempt worker earns 1.5 times the pay rate for hours beyond 40 in a week, which is overtime. Most salaried employees are exempt and receive nothing extra for long weeks, which is why converting a salary to its true hourly rate is useful before accepting a role with heavy hours. The federal minimum wage is a floor, and many states set a higher one. Unions often bargain for premium pay on a holiday shift, so check your contract before assuming the standard overtime math applies.

        A bonus is usually withheld at the flat supplemental wage rate of 22% rather than your regular rate, so it can look smaller than expected on the day it is paid even if the annual return reconciles at filing time. Self-employed contractors receive no benefits and pay both halves of FICA, so they typically need a higher hourly rate than a salaried colleague just to match the same take-home.

        State Income Tax and Local Taxes: Where Your Net Income Shifts

        Geography moves your net income more than most people expect. Nine states levy no state income tax at all, while others tax income at flat or graduated rates. Some cities and counties add local taxes on top, and several states withhold for state disability insurance, paid family leave or the unemployment tax. The same salary can therefore produce a different state tax bill depending on where you work, not where your employer is headquartered.

        To keep the estimate honest, enter a state income tax rate that reflects your location, and treat the result as an approximation. A person who is tax-exempt from federal withholding on the W-4, because they owed no tax last year and expect none this year, should leave that choice to the calculator.

        Reading Your Pay Stub After Using the Salary Paycheck Calculator

        A pay stub lists the pay period dates, hours worked, gross pay, every withholding and deduction, net pay and year-to-date totals. Compare it line by line with the estimate from the salary paycheck calculator. A mismatch usually has one of these causes:

        • A different pay frequency than the one you entered
        • A benefit deduction that is pre-tax on your stub but post-tax in the estimate
        • A state or local tax rate that is not what you assumed
        • Extra W-4 withholding you requested

        Your employer's payroll team can explain any line that still looks wrong, and your per week, per month and per hour figures should reconcile with the annual total.

        Using Your Paycheck Estimate for Job Offers, Budgets and a Pay Raise

        Before accepting an offer, run both salaries through the same paycheck taxes logic and compare the net pay, not the headline. A pay raise of 5% on $71,340 adds $3,567 before taxes, but at a 12% marginal rate plus 7.65% FICA only about $2,866 reaches you. Build your monthly budget from the take-home pay figure, set aside earnings for retirement, and recheck the estimate every time your pay rate, filing status or insurance plan changes.

        Salary Calculator questions

        What does a salary calculator do?

        It converts one pay amount into the others (hourly, daily, weekly, bi-weekly, semi-monthly, monthly, quarterly and annual) and estimates the take-home pay left after taxes and deductions.

        What is the difference between gross pay and net pay?

        Gross pay is what you earn before anything is withheld. Net pay, or take-home pay, is what remains after taxes, pre-tax deductions such as 401(k) and health premiums, and post-tax deductions.

        How do adjusted and unadjusted salaries differ?

        An unadjusted figure treats every weekday as a paid working day. An adjusted figure subtracts holidays and paid vacation days from the working days, so each hour you actually work is worth more.

        What is the difference between bi-weekly and semi-monthly pay?

        Bi-weekly pay arrives every other week, giving 26 paychecks a year. Semi-monthly pay arrives twice a month, giving 24 paychecks, so each semi-monthly check is larger.

        How do I calculate annual salary from an hourly wage?

        Multiply the hourly wage by the hours you work each week and by 52 weeks. Holidays and unpaid time off lower the real yearly amount, so count only the weeks you are paid.

        Which taxes come out of a paycheck?

        Federal income tax based on your W-4, Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) under FICA, plus any state or local income taxes and state insurance programs that apply where you work.

        Is a bonus taxed differently from regular pay?

        Often yes. Employers commonly withhold federal tax on a bonus at a flat supplemental rate, so the withholding can look higher than on a regular paycheck even if the yearly tax settles at filing.

        Is this take-home pay figure exact?

        No. It is an estimate based on 2026 federal brackets, the standard deduction and the rates you enter for state and local tax. Your employer's payroll system and your W-4 determine the actual withholding.
