        How a Take-Home Paycheck Calculator Works from Gross Pay to Net Income

        Every paycheck calculator performs the same gross-to-net conversion. It starts with what your employer agrees to pay you, subtracts the taxes your employer has to withhold for the IRS and your state, removes the benefit contributions you elected, and shows what is left as your net income. That remainder is your final paycheck, the amount you can spend, and the one that matters for budgeting.

        The calculation is a simple subtraction chain, even though each term inside it takes some work to compute:

        $$\text{Net pay} = \text{Gross pay} - \text{Pre-tax deductions} - \text{FICA} - \text{Federal income tax} - \text{State income tax} - \text{Post-tax deductions}$$

        Because the result is what you keep, it is also called after-tax income or disposable income. Mortgage lenders and job offers quote your pre-tax figure, while your grocery and rent budget depends on the after-tax one.

        Gross Pay vs. Take Home Pay

        Gross pay is your salary or hourly earnings before anything is removed; in tax language it is your gross income. Take home pay is what remains after withholding and deductions. For a salaried employee, gross pay per paycheck is your annual salary divided by the number of pay periods:

        $$\text{Gross per paycheck} = \frac{\text{Annual salary}}{\text{Pay periods per year}}$$

        Who Uses a Take-Home Paycheck Calculator

        • An employee comparing job offers that quote a salary but not the after-tax amount
        • A new hire who wants to preview a first paycheck before the pay stub arrives
        • A household building a monthly budget from take home salary rather than gross income
        • An employer or payroll manager estimating what staff will see after taxes and benefit deductions

        Some sites spell the tool name as a "take-home-paycheck calculator", but the idea is identical: salary in, then net pay per paycheck and per year out.

        Salary Paycheck Calculator Inputs: What to Enter

        A salary paycheck calculator only needs a handful of facts, and each one maps to a line on your pay stub. Entering them accurately matters more than anything else you do with the tool.

        1. Annual salary or hourly wage – the pre-tax amount, never what you think you take home.
        2. Pay frequency – weekly, bi-weekly, semi-monthly or monthly.
        3. Filing status – single, married filing jointly, married filing separately or head of household.
        4. State and locality – decides your state income tax and any local tax.
        5. Pre-tax deductions – 401(k) contributions, health insurance premiums, HSA or FSA contributions.
        6. Post-tax deductions – Roth 401(k) contributions, union dues or other items taken after taxes.
        7. Additional withholding – any extra dollar amount you ask your employer to withhold.

        Filing Status and the Standard Deduction

        Your filing status determines which tax brackets and which standard deduction apply to your wages. These are the federal amounts for the 2026 tax year:

        Filing statusWho it fitsStandard deduction
        SingleUnmarried, divorced or legally separated$16,100
        Married filing jointlyA married couple filing one return$32,200
        Married filing separatelySpouses who file two returns$16,100
        Head of householdUnmarried and paying more than half the cost of a home for a qualifying person$24,150
        Qualifying widow(er)Has a dependent child after a spouse's death$32,200

        Most people choose single, married filing jointly or head of household. If you have itemized deductions larger than the standard deduction, such as mortgage interest or charitable giving, you may benefit from itemizing, though withholding on a paycheck normally assumes the standard amount.

        Form W-4 and Dependents

        Your employer calculates federal withholding from the Form W-4 you submit. The current W-4 no longer uses allowances. It asks for your filing status, whether you hold multiple jobs, your dependents and any other income or adjustments, and it lets you request additional withholding per pay period. Resubmit your W-4 after a marriage, a new child or a second job so the tool and your payroll stay aligned.

        Federal Paycheck Calculator Logic: Income Tax Brackets and Withholding

        A federal paycheck calculator applies a progressive tax: each slice of your taxable income is charged at its own tax rate, so only the top slice pays the highest marginal rate. Taxable income is your wages minus pre-tax deductions and the standard deduction. These are the 2026 federal tax brackets for a single filer:

        Federal Income Tax Withholding

        Federal income tax is usually the largest single deduction on a paycheck. Your employer withholds it gradually all year, and the amount depends on your W-4, so tax withholding only approximates your final tax liability. When withholding is too high you receive a tax refund in the spring; when it is too low, you owe the difference. Trimming withholding enlarges every paycheck but raises the chance of a bill from the IRS.

        Tax Credits and the One Big Beautiful Bill

        Credits reduce tax directly rather than shrinking your taxable income, so the tax credits you plan to claim can justify a lower withholding. For tax years 2025 through 2028, the One Big Beautiful Bill also added deductions for qualified tips, overtime pay, car loan interest and a bonus deduction for seniors, each of which phases out at higher incomes. The paycheck estimate leaves these filing-time items out, so they change your refund rather than the per-paycheck net the tool shows.

        FICA Taxes in Your Paycheck Calculator: Social Security and Medicare

        FICA stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act, and it is the payroll tax that funds retirement and health programs. It is mandatory and it is withheld from the first dollar you earn, with no standard deduction to shelter it.

        Social Security Tax and the Wage Base Limit

        Your Social Security contribution is 6.2% of wages, and your employer adds a matching 6.2%. The Social Security tax applies only up to the wage base limit, which is $184,500 for 2026. Once your year-to-date earnings pass that cap, this withholding stops for the rest of the year, which is why some high earners see a larger final paycheck in December.

        Medicare Tax and Additional Medicare Tax

        The Medicare tax is 1.45% of every dollar of wages, with no cap, and your employer matches it. Higher earners also owe the Additional Medicare tax of 0.9%, which only the employee pays, once wages exceed these thresholds:

        Filing statusAdditional Medicare threshold
        Single$200,000
        Head of household$200,000
        Married filing jointly$250,000
        Married filing separately$125,000

        If you work for yourself, the self-employment tax combines both halves, 15.3% in total, because you are both employee and employer. The calculator models employee pay, so it does not include this tax.

        Pre-Tax and Post-Tax Deductions That Change Your Take-Home Pay

        After taxes, deductions are the second reason your take-home pay falls short of your gross pay. Some come out before taxes are calculated, others afterward, and the order changes your bill.

        Pre-Tax Deductions

        Pre-tax deductions lower the wages that income tax is based on, so they cost you less than their face value. The common ones are:

        • Traditional 401(k)s and 403(b) plans, whose retirement contributions, which grow tax-deferred
        • Employer-sponsored health insurance premiums and other pretax benefit deductions
        • A health savings account (HSA) or flexible spending account (FSA) for medical costs
        • Commuter benefits and dependent care accounts

        Post-Tax Deductions

        Post-tax deductions come out after income taxes are applied, so they do not reduce your taxable income. Roth 401(k) contributions are the usual example, and they grow tax-free because you already paid the tax. Court-ordered wage garnishments for child support, unpaid taxes or defaulted loans also come out of your pay, and garnishments are applied after taxes are withheld. The calculator treats short-term disability and state unemployment tax charges, where your state levies them, as additional state deductions.

        Pricing an Apartment with a Paycheck Calculator on Semi-Monthly Pay

        Dana, a surveyor in Austin, has an offer at $73,860 a year, paid semi-monthly, and two apartments to choose between: one at $1,640 a month and one at $1,495. The landlord's income test uses gross pay, but Dana wants to know what the rent looks like against money that actually arrives.

        Dana opens the paycheck calculator and enters $73,860 as the salary, selects semi-monthly, picks single, chooses Texas (no state income tax) and adds a pre-tax health savings account contribution of $150 per paycheck. The tool divides the salary into 24 checks of $3,077.50, then subtracts $150.00 for the HSA, $181.50 for Social Security, $42.45 for Medicare and $260.47 of federal income tax. The take-home figure reads $2,443.08 per paycheck, which is $4,886.16 a month.

        • $1,640 rent is 26.6% of gross pay, which clears a typical 30% landlord screen, but it is 33.6% of the $4,886.16 Dana actually takes home.
        • $1,495 rent is 30.6% of take-home pay.

        Dana then checks the result against the 50/30/20 budgeting guideline, which caps needs at 50% of after-tax income: $2,443.08 a month, exactly one paycheck. At $1,495, rent leaves $948.08 for groceries, insurance, a phone bill and a car payment; at $1,640 only $803.08 remains. Dana rents the $1,495 unit and reruns the calculator with the HSA set to $100 to see whether trimming it would justify the larger apartment. Net pay rises by about $80 a month, which is not enough to close the $145 rent gap, so the cheaper unit stands.

        Pay Frequency and Take Home Pay: Bi-Weekly vs. Semi-Monthly

        Your pay frequency changes the size of each check but not your annual total. Bi-weekly pay means every other week, giving 26 paychecks a year; semi-monthly pay means two fixed dates a month, giving 24. A bi-weekly worker gets two extra-large months a year with three paychecks. Using the worked example below, here is how one salary splits:

        Pay frequencyPaychecks per yearGross per paycheckNet per paycheck
        Weekly52$1,296.15$909.87
        Bi-weekly26$2,592.31$1,819.73
        Semi-monthly24$2,808.33$1,971.38
        Monthly12$5,616.67$3,942.76

        Pay frequency does not affect your tax liability; it only divides the same yearly total into more or fewer pieces. Pick the frequency that matches your pay stub, because the tool uses it to divide your annual net into individual checks.

        State Income Tax and Local Tax: Why Net Income Varies by Location

        Two people with the same salary and the same filing status can keep very different net income depending on where they work. Because income taxes are charged at both levels, the state income tax ranges from nothing in states such as Texas, Florida, Washington and Wyoming to more than 13% in California. A handful of states charge a flat rate, including Illinois, Colorado and Pennsylvania, while others use progressive brackets.

        Local Tax and Other State Withholding

        A local tax applies in some cities and counties, and large cities tend to have the highest rates. Some states also withhold for disability insurance, paid family leave and the state unemployment tax. That is why a paycheck calculator asks for your state and, where it applies, your city.

        Hourly Paycheck Calculator Mode: Hourly Wage, Overtime and Tips

        An hourly paycheck calculator replaces annual salary with your hourly wage and the hours worked in the pay period. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, non-exempt employees must earn overtime of at least one and a half times their regular rate for hours past 40 in a week, while exempt salaried staff generally do not. The tool therefore asks for regular hours and overtime hours separately.

        The conversion from an hourly rate to an annual salary is a quick estimate:

        $$\text{Annual salary} \approx \text{Hourly wage} \times \text{Hours per week} \times 52$$

        Reported tips count as wages for withholding, although the One Big Beautiful Bill's deduction for qualified tips can lower what you owe at filing time. If your paycheck is paid to a paycard rather than a bank account, the taxes are calculated the same way, so you enter the same inputs in the tool.

        Worked Example: Take-Home Pay on a $67,400 Annual Salary

        Here is a complete bi-weekly calculation for a single filer who earns $67,400 a year, contributes 6% to a traditional 401(k), pays $118.75 per paycheck for pre-tax health coverage and lives in a state with a flat 4.95% income tax rate. The model is simplified: it applies the standard deduction and ignores local taxes and credits.

        1. Gross per paycheck: \(67{,}400 \div 26 = \$2{,}592.31\).
        2. Pre-tax deductions: 6% to the 401(k) is $155.54, plus $118.75 for health insurance, for a combined $274.29.
        3. FICA: Social Security is $153.36 and Medicare is $35.87 on wages after the health premium, which is $189.23 in total.
        4. Federal income tax: taxable income is $67,400 − $4,044.00 − $3,087.50 − $16,100 = $44,168.50, taxed at 10% on the first $12,400 and 12% on the remaining $31,768.50, for $5,052.22 a year, or $194.32 per paycheck.
        5. State income tax: 4.95% of wages after pre-tax deductions, or $114.74 per paycheck.

        The result: $1,819.73 per paycheck, or 70.2% of gross pay. Those 26 bi-weekly paychecks add up to the same yearly total as any other schedule. Notice that the effective federal rate is only 7.5% even though the top slice of income is taxed at 12%, which is the whole point of a progressive system.

        Checking the Result Against Your Pay Stub

        A pay stub lists the pay period dates, hours worked, gross pay, each tax withheld, every deduction, year-to-date totals and your take-home pay. Compare it with the calculator line by line. A small difference is normal, because payroll systems round differently and use their own withholding tables. A large gap usually means a W-4 entry, a benefit election or a local tax is missing from the inputs.

        How to Increase Your Take Home Salary

        Once you see the numbers, you can decide what to change. These are the levers that move your net pay.

        • Negotiate a raise or bonus. Moving to a new job usually brings a bigger jump than an internal promotion; enter the new salary in the tool to see the net gain.
        • Review benefits. Cheaper insurance tiers reduce the premium removed from each check, and one spouse's plan may cover the whole family.
        • Use an HSA or FSA. Contributions lower your taxable income, so you pay for qualified expenses with pre-tax money.
        • Work overtime. Non-exempt staff earn at least time and a half beyond 40 hours.
        • Cash out paid time off. Some employers convert unused paid time off (PTO) into pay; add that payout as extra gross pay in the tool and rerun it to see the net gain.
        • Adjust your withholding. A bigger W-4 adjustment raises every check but may shrink your refund.

        Pausing retirement contributions is a last resort; if money is tight, keep contributing at least enough to earn your employer's full match.

        Budgeting and Savings from Your Net Pay

        Build your budget from net pay, not gross. Divide your expenses, including rent, insurance and debt payments, against the $1,819.73 figure, then direct a fixed share of each of your paychecks to savings. Budgeting from your gross income leads people to overestimate what they can spend, which is the most common mistake the calculator is meant to prevent.

        Take-Home Paycheck Calculator questions

        What is take-home pay?

        Take-home pay, also called net pay, is the amount you receive after your employer withholds federal, state and payroll taxes and any benefit or retirement deductions from your gross pay.

        Why is my paycheck smaller than my salary divided by pay periods?

        Your employer withholds federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare and often state or local tax, plus deductions such as health insurance and 401(k) contributions, before paying you.

        How do pre-tax and post-tax deductions differ?

        Pre-tax deductions, such as a traditional 401(k), HSA or health premium, lower the income used to calculate income tax. Post-tax deductions, such as Roth 401(k) contributions, come out after taxes and do not.

        Does pay frequency change how much I take home in a year?

        No. Weekly, bi-weekly, semi-monthly and monthly schedules split the same annual pay into different numbers of checks, so each check is smaller or larger but the yearly total is the same.

        How is Social Security and Medicare tax calculated?

        Employees pay 6.2% Social Security tax on wages up to the annual wage base ($184,500 for 2026) and 1.45% Medicare tax on all wages, plus 0.9% Additional Medicare tax above income thresholds that depend on filing status.

        Is this calculator exact?

        It is an estimate. Payroll systems use their own withholding tables, and this tool applies 2026 federal brackets with a flat state rate you enter, so your actual paycheck can differ slightly.

        How can I increase my take-home pay?

        You can earn more through a raise, bonus or overtime, lower costly benefit deductions, use an HSA or FSA to reduce taxable income, or adjust the withholding on your Form W-4.

        Does the calculator handle hourly and overtime pay?

        Yes. Switch Pay type to Hourly, then enter your hourly wage, regular hours, overtime wage and overtime hours for the pay period.
