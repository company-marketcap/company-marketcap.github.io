        How the Taxable vs. Tax-Deferred Investments Calculator Works

        The investment growth calculator runs the same savings plan through three account types at once: a fully taxable account, a tax-deferred account and a tax-free account. Everything is identical between the three except when the tax is collected, which is exactly why the results differ. Each field below matches one input of the tool. The charitable giving tax savings calculator online is free to use with no sign-up, and works on desktop and mobile.

        Annual rate of return

        This is the yearly return you expect after fund costs. The rate of return compounds every month in the model, and for the taxable account it is reduced by your tax rate on earnings. A cautious figure of 5% to 7% suits a diversified portfolio, because actual volatility from year to year can be much wider than the average. For context, the S&P 500 has delivered a compounded rate of return near 11% over several decades, but never in a straight line. A higher return widens the gap between the taxable and tax-deferred balances, because tax removes a share of every extra gain.

        Years to contribute and years of withdrawals

        The years to contribute field sets how long you keep adding money, and years of withdrawals sets how long the balance pays you back. Enter 1 for a lump sum payout instead of a stream of distributions.

        Existing balance and new contributions

        Your existing balance is what the accounts already hold, and your new contributions are the periodic amount you add. Every principal dollar you add is treated as net contribution money you could have spent, so the three accounts start from the same sacrifice.

        Contribution frequency and withdrawal frequency

        Choose monthly, quarterly or annual timing for both. The contribution frequency controls how soon each deposit starts compounding, while the withdrawal frequency controls how the payout is sliced.

        Tax during contributions and withdrawals

        Enter your estimated marginal tax rate for the contribution years and again for the payout years. Look up your filing status in the current federal income tax table to estimate each one, and remember that state levies add to the figure.

        Tax deduction

        Tick the tax deduction box and the tool assumes tax-deferred contributions are deductible, then raises the deposit so the take-home cost matches the other accounts. That setting is the main reason a tax-deferred plan can beat a tax-free one when you expect a lower bracket in retirement.

        Tax-Deferred Account vs. Tax-Free Account: How Each Is Taxed

        All three choices answer the same question: when do you hand over the tax liability? The table summarizes the tax treatment of each one. Next, open the dividend tax calculator online and enter your own details to see an estimate in seconds.

        Account typeTaxes on contributionsTaxes on growthTaxes on withdrawals
        Taxable accountPaid before you investPaid every yearOnly on the gains
        Tax-deferred accountOften deductiblePostponedTaxed in full
        Tax-free accountPaid before you investNoneNone

        Taxable account

        A taxable account such as a brokerage account has no shelter. Interest and dividends are taxed in the year they arrive, and capital gains are taxed when you sell, so a slice of the yearly gain leaves before it can compound. That drag is the reason the calculator shows the taxable line trailing the others.

        Tax-deferred account

        A tax-deferred account postpones the bill. Your deposits can lower taxable income now, the balance grows without yearly tax, and each payout is taxed as ordinary income. A traditional 401(k) is the classic example, and pre-tax deposits reduce your current-year income. Early withdrawals can also trigger penalties.

        Tax-free account

        A tax-free account, also called tax-exempt, is funded with money you already paid taxes on. In return you owe nothing on qualified payouts, and a Roth account has no required minimum distributions for its original owner. The calculator treats that treatment as the benchmark, since its money never faces a second tax.

        Formula: Taxable vs. Tax Deferred vs. Tax Free Growth

        The model compounds monthly, so each account’s balance \(B\) after \(n\) months follows one recurrence with a different growth rate \(g\). The taxable account uses a reduced rate because yearly tax removes part of each gain:

        $$B_{n} = (B_{n-1} + C)\times(1 + g)$$

        $$g_{taxable} = \frac{r \times (1 - t_{c})}{12} \qquad g_{deferred} = g_{free} = \frac{r}{12}$$

        The annual balance at the end of the saving years is then paid out as a level stream. The tax-deferred payout is cut by the withdrawal tax rate, while the tax-free payout is not:

        $$W = B \times \frac{g}{1 - (1+g)^{-m}} \qquad W_{deferred}^{net} = W \times (1 - t_{w})$$

        Here \(r\) is the annual return, \(C\) the periodic deposit, \(t_{c}\) the tax rate during contributions, \(t_{w}\) the rate during withdrawals and \(m\) the number of payouts. A tax-deferred plan comes out ahead of a taxable one whenever \(t_{w}\) is low enough that the one-time bill is smaller than the yearly taxes avoided.

        Why the tax-deferred and tax-free balances match

        Both accounts compound at the full 6.8%. The gap appears only at withdrawal, when the tax-deferred payout loses 22% to income tax. If your rate during retirement equals your rate while saving and you can deduct deposits, the two end up even.

        How the withdrawal rate changes the winner

        Raise the payout tax rate to 37% and the taxable plan’s $1,773 beats the tax-deferred plan’s $1,732 after tax, while lowering it to 12% lifts the tax-deferred payout to $2,420. Rerun the form with your own rates before you commit.

        Which Tax-Advantaged Accounts Count as Tax-Deferred or Tax-Free

        Real accounts map onto the three columns of the calculator. Knowing which is which turns the output into an action.

        • A traditional IRA, 401(k) or 403(b) is tax-deferred, usually funded with pre-tax pay through an employer plan.
        • A Roth IRA or Roth 401(k) is tax-free, since contributions go in after tax and qualified earnings come out untaxed.
        • A health savings account (HSA) can be tax-free for qualified medical expenses, with deductible deposits and untaxed growth.

        Annual contribution limits apply to each, and people over 50 can add catch-up contributions. A taxable account has no cap, so it holds whatever you save beyond those limits, and the calculator’s taxable column shows what that overflow is worth after yearly tax on dividends, reinvestment and the gains from common investment vehicles. Check the current limits before you size your new contributions.

        Comparing a Taxable, Tax-Deferred and Tax-Free Plan at a 32% Bracket

        A 41-year-old engineer with $64,300 already saved has a new $1,150 a month to invest and two choices at work: a traditional 401(k) or a Roth 401(k). Her top federal bracket today is 32%, and she expects to retire in 19 years and draw income for 20 more, probably in the 24% bracket that starts at $105,700 for a single filer. She opens the taxable vs. tax-deferred comparison and enters a 7.1% return, 32% during contributions, 24% during withdrawals, then clicks the calculate button.

        The first run leaves the deduction box unticked. The taxable account ends at $590,512 and pays $4,027 a month. The tax-deferred and tax-free accounts both reach $801,732 and pay $6,264 a month before tax, but the tax-deferred one drops to $4,761 after a 24% bill, while the tax-free account keeps all $6,264. On that view the Roth looks $1,503 a month better.

        That comparison is unfair to the traditional plan, because she would not deposit the same $1,150 after tax into it. She ticks the deduction box, so deposits are raised to the pre-tax amount that costs her the same take-home pay. The tax-deferred balance climbs to $1,062,875 and the after-tax payout to $6,311 a month, $47 more than the Roth’s $6,264. The 32% deduction now outweighs the 24% bill later.

        She also reruns the form with 32% in retirement. The tax-deferred payout falls to $4,260 after tax, well below the Roth, which tells her the choice only wins while her bracket drops. The taxable column, at $4,027 a month, is well behind both. Her decision: send the $1,150 to the traditional 401(k) and revisit the split if a pension or a large rollover could lift her retirement income above the 24% range.

        Retirement Planning: Choosing Between Tax-Deferred and Tax-Free Investment

        Good retirement planning starts with your current and expected future bracket. If you expect a higher bracket later, the tax-free investment usually wins, because you pay today’s lower rate once. If you expect a lower bracket later, a tax-deferred account usually wins, because the deduction is worth more than the later bill.

        Most people do not need to pick only one, and the calculator is how you test a split: run it once with all deposits in the tax-deferred column, once with all in the tax-free column, and compare the totals to see how much each dollar moved between them is worth. A taxable account adds liquidity because you can reach it without age restrictions. Remember that inflation erodes every payout, that the result carries risk because returns are hypothetical, and that a financial advisor can review the numbers against your full picture.

        Use the tax benefits of each account in order: capture any employer match first, then compare the tax-free investment and tax-deferred routes with this tool, and put the rest of your savings in a taxable account. Print the calculator’s report to compare scenarios side by side, then update the figures whenever your tax rates or goals change. A taxable vs. tax-deferred vs. tax-free calculator only estimates, so treat the gap between accounts as a direction rather than a promise. A taxable vs. tax-deferred calculator is just as useful for testing a rate you are unsure about.

        Taxable vs. Tax-Deferred Investments Calculator questions

        What is the difference between taxable and tax-deferred investments?

        In a taxable account you pay tax on interest, dividends and gains as they arise. In a tax-deferred account, such as a traditional IRA or 401(k), growth is untaxed until you withdraw, and then the payout is taxed as ordinary income.

        Is tax-deferred or tax-free better?

        It depends on your tax rate now compared with later. If you expect a lower bracket in retirement, tax-deferred usually wins. If you expect a higher bracket, a tax-free account such as a Roth usually wins. Use the two tax rate fields to test both.

        What does the tax deduction checkbox do?

        It assumes tax-deferred contributions are deductible and raises the deposit so the take-home cost matches the taxable and tax-free accounts. Leave it unticked to compare equal deposits instead.

        Which tax rate should I enter?

        Use your estimated marginal federal rate, plus any state rate, for the contribution years and again for the withdrawal years. Your filing status and taxable income decide the bracket.

        Why does the taxable account grow more slowly?

        Tax is taken from earnings each year in a taxable account, so a smaller balance compounds. Lower capital gains and dividend rates would narrow the gap shown here.

        Does this calculator include fees or inflation?

        No. Rates of return are before fund costs, and all dollar amounts are in future dollars. Use a lower return to approximate fees or inflation.

        Are the results a guarantee?

        No. Returns, tax laws and your income will change, so treat the output as a hypothetical comparison and consult a financial advisor for personal advice.
