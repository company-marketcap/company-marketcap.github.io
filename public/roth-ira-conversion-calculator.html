        How a Roth IRA Conversion Changes Your Net Worth

        A Roth IRA conversion moves pre-tax money out of a traditional IRA and into a Roth IRA. You owe income tax on the converted amount in the year you convert, and in exchange the Roth grows tax-free. Qualified withdrawals later are not taxed at all, while every dollar you pull from a traditional IRA is taxed as ordinary income. That trade is the whole decision: pay tax now at your current rate, or pay tax later at whatever rate applies in retirement. Next, open the roth vs pre-tax account calculator online and enter your own details to see an estimate in seconds.

        Because the tax consequences land in two different years, comparing balances alone is misleading. A $100,000 traditional IRA and a $100,000 Roth IRA are not worth the same amount, since the first carries deferred taxes. The fair comparison converts both to after-tax dollars at the same retirement date, which is exactly what a calculator does.

        Why your marginal tax rate matters most

        Your marginal tax rate is the rate applied to your last dollar of income, and it is the rate a conversion is taxed at. If the rate you pay on the conversion is lower than the rate you expect on withdrawals, you tend to win when you convert your traditional IRA. If it is higher, you usually come out ahead by waiting. Growth and timeframe only scale the gap; they do not flip the answer.

        Tax-free growth and your earnings

        Once the money sits in a Roth, all earnings compound without a tax drag. A longer timeframe magnifies the tax-rate gap, because the same gap is applied to a larger final balance. That is why a conversion in your forties often has more to gain than one at sixty-five.

        The Roth IRA Conversion Calculator Formula

        Behind every Roth IRA conversion calculator are two future values measured after tax. For the traditional IRA you grow the starting account balance at the annual rate of return and then subtract the tax you will owe on withdrawal: The free 457 roth vs pre-tax calculator uses the same plain-English approach, so you can compare results side by side.

        $$FV_{trad} = B \times (1 + r)^{n} \times (1 - t_{ret})$$

        For the converted account you subtract tax at the current tax rate first, then grow what is left:

        $$FV_{roth} = B \times (1 - t_{now}) \times (1 + r)^{n}$$

        Here \(B\) is the amount to convert, \(r\) the yearly return, \(n\) the years until your targeted retirement age, \(t_{now}\) your tax rate on the conversion and \(t_{ret}\) the rate you expect later. Subtract one from the other and the result simplifies to \(B \times (1 + r)^{n} \times (t_{ret} - t_{now})\), which is why the tax-rate gap decides the outcome.

        Paying the tax out of pocket

        If you cover the conversion tax with cash from outside the account, the whole balance stays invested. A fair comparison assumes that cash would otherwise have grown at the same rate, so the out of pocket and paid-from-balance versions of the calculation land on the same difference. Either way the net-worth gain is the same; the practical benefit of paying from savings is that more money sits inside the Roth, where it shelters future growth.

        Inputs That Drive a Roth Conversion

        Each field in the tool maps to a variable above, so it helps to know what to enter and how sensitive the result is to each one.

        • Starting account balance: the traditional IRA value you could convert, all of it or a portion.
        • Current tax rate: the marginal rate you would pay on the conversion, which includes the extra income the conversion adds.
        • Retirement tax rate: your best estimate of the rate on withdrawals, using your expected income level in retirement.
        • Annual rate of return: the growth you assume; the result is a projection, not a guarantee.
        • Retirement age: the date the comparison is measured at, which sets the timeframe.

        Change one input at a time. Raising the return or the timeframe stretches both outcomes, but only a different tax rate can reverse which account wins.

        Running a Roth Conversion Through a Sabbatical Year

        Tomasz, 51, is taking eight months unpaid to restore a sailboat, so his taxable income this year will land well below normal. His traditional IRA holds $212,350, and he is deciding whether to use the lean year to convert $41,800 of it. He opens the calculator and enters $41,800 as the amount to convert, 12% as the current rate (the federal bracket his reduced income falls into), 22% as the rate he expects in retirement, 5.8% a year, and a retirement age of 65, which gives a 14-year timeframe.

        The calculator reports a conversion tax of $5,016, leaving $36,784 to grow. At 65 the traditional route is worth $71,791 after tax, while the converted Roth reaches $80,995, a lead of $9,204. Because the rate gap is 10 points and the balance more than doubles, the Roth edge is large.

        Before committing, he reruns the calculator with 24% in the current-rate field, which is what the extra income would cost if the conversion pushed him into the next bracket. The result flips to a $1,841 deficit for the Roth. That tells him the exact limit: the current-rate input has to stay at 12%, so the conversion must stay inside that bracket, and he checks his projected taxable income against the bracket ceiling, subtracts it from the ceiling and caps the amount to convert at that headroom. He also confirms with his tax advisor that he has $5,016 in a savings account for the bill, so the tax does not come out of the IRA itself. The next step is a smaller second conversion in the following tax year if any headroom remains.

        Eligibility for a Traditional to Roth IRA Conversion

        Since 2010 there has been no income cap on a traditional to Roth IRA conversion, so anyone who is eligible to hold a traditional IRA can convert. Pre-tax money in an employer-sponsored plan such as a 401(k), 403(b) or governmental 457(b) can also move through a qualified rollover. Once the money is in a converted Roth IRA, you can also skip required minimum distributions during your lifetime.

        If you also hold after-tax or non-deductible contributions, the pro-rata rule treats all your traditional IRA balances as one pool, which changes how much of a conversion is taxable.

        Taxes, the Five-Year Rule and Withdrawals

        Withdrawals of converted money are tax-free when the account has been open five years and you are 591/2 or older, or when another qualifying event such as death or disability applies. Taking converted funds out too early can trigger penalties, so a conversion works best with money you will not need soon. A tax advisor can confirm how the added income affects credits, Medicare premiums and your bracket before you commit.

        Spreading a conversion over several years

        Converting in slices keeps each year's income below the next bracket. A low-income year, such as a career gap or early retirement, is the classic window for a larger Roth conversion, because the deferred taxes are paid at an unusually low rate. Rerun the calculator for each slice, entering that slice as the amount to convert and its bracket rate as the current tax rate, and add up the results.

        Advantages and Disadvantages of an IRA Conversion

        In the calculator, the advantages show up as the Roth's higher after-tax future value whenever your retirement rate exceeds your current rate, and the disadvantages show up as the upfront tax and a negative result when it does not. Tax-free growth and no required minimum distributions add to that edge but are not in the formula. Whether the answer fits your retirement savings goals is a question for your financial advisor and your financial plan; any investment held inside the account stays the same after converting. Revisit the result as your income, growth assumptions and contributions change.

        Before acting, write down your current tax rate, your expected retirement bracket and the cash available to pay the bill from a taxable account. If you cannot pay the tax from outside the IRA, a smaller conversion is usually safer. The comparison is only as good as the rates you feed in, so rerun it with a few taxes scenarios.

        Roth IRA Conversion Calculator questions

        What does a Roth IRA conversion calculator compare?

        It compares the after-tax future value of leaving money in a traditional IRA with the value of the same money after you convert it to a Roth IRA and pay the tax today, measured at your retirement age.

        When does converting to a Roth IRA make sense?

        Usually when your tax rate on the conversion is lower than the rate you expect on withdrawals, for example in a low-income year. If your current rate is higher than your retirement rate, waiting tends to win.

        Is there an income limit on a Roth conversion?

        No. Since 2010 anyone with a traditional IRA, or pre-tax money in an eligible employer plan, can convert regardless of income.

        Should I pay the conversion tax from the IRA or from savings?

        Paying from savings keeps more money inside the Roth, where it grows tax-free. Paying from the converted balance shrinks the amount invested and, if you are under 59½, can trigger a 10% early-withdrawal penalty on the part withheld.

        What is the five-year rule for converted money?

        Converted amounts generally need to sit for five years, or you must be 59½ or older, to avoid a 10% penalty on withdrawing the converted principal. Earnings have their own qualified-withdrawal requirements.

        Does the result guarantee my future balance?

        No. It is a projection from the rate of return and tax rates you enter, not a guarantee of future performance. Laws and brackets can change.

        Can I convert only part of my traditional IRA?

        Yes. Many people convert in slices over several years to stay within a tax bracket. Enter each slice as the amount to convert and run the comparison again.
