        Whether you are settling up after a quiet dinner or a loud birthday table, a tip calculator turns a receipt into a fair gratuity in seconds: enter the bill, choose a percentage, and see the tip amount, the total with tip and what each person owes. Behind that single result sit a few decisions that change the number, such as whether the subtotal or the tax-inclusive total is the base, how many people share the check, and whether a service charge is already printed on the bill. This guide explains each of them with one fully worked example.

        How the Tip Calculator Works

        The tool takes a short list of inputs and returns a short list of results. Everything it does is multiplication and division, which is why you can reproduce any answer by hand once you know the pattern. The inputs are the bill amount, the tip percentage you want to leave, an optional tax line, and the number of people splitting the check. The outputs are the tip amount, the total with tip, the tip per person and the total per person.

        Bill amount, tip percentage and number of people

        Start with the figure printed on your check. If the receipt lists a food subtotal and a separate tax line, enter them separately so the tool can show you both versions of the tip. Next, choose a tip percentage: most diners in the United States land between 15% and 20% for a sit-down meal, while counter service might justify only 10% or a couple of dollars. Finally, enter the party size if you want an even split. Quick presets such as 15%, 18% and 20% cover most meals, and a custom tip field handles anything else.

        • Bill: the pre-tax subtotal, or the full amount if your receipt shows only one line.
        • Tip percentage: your reaction to the service, from 10% for a disappointing visit to 25% for an outstanding one.
        • Tax: optional, entered only when you want the tip based on the pre-tax figure.
        • Number of people: used to divide the total evenly.

        Pre-tax vs post-tax: choosing the tip base

        The tip base is the amount your percentage is applied to. Many diners tip on the pre-tax subtotal, reasoning that sales tax is a government charge and not part of the meal your server delivered. Other diners, and some point-of-sale systems that print suggested tips, use the post-tax total, which makes the gratuity a little larger. Neither is a rule, and whether to leave a tip on tax at all is a matter of etiquette and personal judgment, entirely discretionary. The tool lets you switch the base so you can match whichever convention you follow, and the worked example below shows exactly how much the choice matters on a real check.

        Quick check: if your receipt already prints suggested tip amounts, look at whether they were calculated before or after tax, then mirror that choice so your numbers agree with theirs.

        How to Calculate a Tip: Formulas

        Learning how to calculate a tip takes one formula. The tip is the tip base multiplied by the percentage written as a decimal, and the total is everything added together:

        $$\text{Tip} = \text{Base} \times \frac{\text{Tip \%}}{100}$$

        $$\text{Total} = \text{Subtotal} + \text{Tax} + \text{Tip}$$

        $$\text{Per person} = \frac{\text{Total}}{\text{Number of people}}$$

        The one-step multiplier

        If you only want the final amount to pay, you can skip the separate tip line and multiply the total bill by one plus the decimal percentage. Multiply the check amount once, and you have the final figure. A 20% gratuity becomes a multiplier of 1.20, and an 18% gratuity becomes 1.18. A bill of $48.00 with an 18% tip is therefore \(48.00 \times 1.18 = 56.64\), and the tip itself is \(48.00 \times 0.18 = 8.64\). Use the multiplier when the tax is already inside the bill amount and you do not need the tip shown separately.

        Removing a service charge from the bill

        Some restaurants add a service charge or auto-gratuity to larger tables. To see what the bill would have been without it, divide the printed total by one plus the charge rate. A total of $184.30 that includes a 20% service charge was $184.30 ÷ 1.20 = $153.58 before the charge. That also tells you whether the printed gratuity already covers what you intended to leave, so you do not tip twice by accident.

        Worked Example with the Restaurant Tip Calculator

        Three friends finish dinner. The receipt shows a food subtotal of $93.40 and 8.25% sales tax, which is $7.71. They agree on an 18% tip on the pre-tax subtotal because the service was attentive but not exceptional. Here is the full calculation.

        StepCalculationResult
        Sales tax$93.40 × 0.0825$7.71
        Tip amount (pre-tax base)$93.40 × 0.18$16.81
        Total with tip$93.40 + $7.71 + $16.81$117.92
        Total per person (3 people)$117.92 ÷ 3$39.31

        Had they used the post-tax base instead, the tip would be \(101.11 \times 0.18 = 18.20\), the total would be $119.31 and each person would pay $39.77. Choosing the pre-tax base saves the table $1.39 overall, which is about 46 cents each. The percentage is the same, and only the base changed, so the difference is small but it is real money over many meals.

        Rounding the share per person

        Three equal shares of $117.92 come to $39.31 each, which adds up to $117.93, one cent over. Rounding like this is normal. Most groups round up to a clean number, such as $40 each, and leave the extra 69 cents as a slightly larger gratuity for the server. A simple round-up like that keeps the payment easy for everyone.

        Calculate Tip in Your Head

        You will not always have a phone out, and a little mental math goes a long way. The method relies on one easy move: shift the decimal point one place to the left to find 10% of the bill.

        1. Find 10% by moving the decimal point one place left. For $93.40 that is $9.34.
        2. Double it for 20%: $9.34 × 2 = $18.68.
        3. Add half of the 10% figure to get 15%: $9.34 + $4.67 = $14.01.
        4. For 18%, take the 20% figure and subtract a tenth of it, which lands near $16.81.

        These shortcuts get you within a few cents of the exact answer, which is plenty at the table. Keep the calculator for splitting a check, adding tax or checking an unusual percentage.

        Working Out the Gratuity and Bill Split for a Team Lunch

        Dana books a Thursday lunch for five teammates, and the receipt lands with a subtotal of $168.35 and 7% sales tax of $11.78. At the bottom, the printer suggests 18%, 20% or 22% tips, so Dana opens the calculator to see whether those suggestions are built on the pre-tax or post-tax amount.

        First entry: bill $168.35, tax $11.78, tip 20% on the pre-tax subtotal, five people. The tip amount comes back as $33.67, the total with tip as $213.80, and each person owes $42.76. Then Dana switches the base to the post-tax total of $180.13. The 20% tip becomes $36.03, which matches the 20% figure on the receipt exactly, so the printer was using the post-tax base. The gap between the two is $2.36 across the table.

        • Printed 18% on the post-tax base: $32.42.
        • Printed 22% on the post-tax base: $39.63.
        • Pre-tax 20%, the figure Dana first entered: $33.67.

        The server handled all five orders alone and recovered quickly from a kitchen error, so the table agrees on the full 20% as the figure to type into the calculator. The standard U.S. range for sit-down dining is 15% to 20%, so 20% sits at its top. Dana picks the post-tax $36.03, so the new total is $216.16 and each share is $43.23. Everyone pays the same, which leaves a single cent to round off, and Dana types that new share into the group chat before the cards come out.

        How Much to Tip for Different Services

        The question of how much to tip depends on who served you and how. In the United States, tipping is customary, and a tip is expected at most sit-down restaurants. The practice is often built into how workers are paid, since the wages of many tipped employees sit below the standard minimum wage and gratuities make up the difference on payroll. That is why tipping culture matters so much to servers, and why this kind of tipping is treated differently from a voluntary gift. Whatever range you settle on, it becomes the percentage you enter in the calculator. The ranges below are practical starting points, not rules, and you should adjust them for exceptional or poor service.

        Restaurants, bars and coffee shops

        • Full-service restaurants: 15% to 20% of the bill for good service, with 18% to 20% common in big cities. The server usually shares the tip with the kitchen and support staff.
        • Bars: $1 to $2 per drink for beer or a glass of wine, or about 20% on cocktails. Bartenders who craft complicated drinks appreciate the higher end.
        • Counter service and coffee: $1 to $2, or around 10% on a large custom order. It is optional, but it is a small gesture of gratitude.
        • Takeout pickup: 0% to 10%, since nobody waited on you.

        Delivery, rides and personal care

        • Food delivery: about 15% of the order or $5 at minimum, more in bad weather or for long distances. The same guidance applies to pizza delivery.
        • Taxi and rideshare: 15% to 20% when you tip in the app.
        • Hotel housekeeping: $1 to $2 per night left daily, because different staff may clean your room on different days.
        • Hairstylist or barber: 15% to 20% of the service price, and a bit more for color work.
        • Tour guide: $5 or more per person for a half-day tour, depending on the length and the quality of service.

        When to tip more, and when to tip less

        Leave a higher percentage after a fine-dining meal, an unusually attentive server, or a large group that kept the waitstaff busy. A good waiter who remembers your preferences earns it. Check the bill first, because many restaurants add an automatic gratuity for large parties, and you rarely need to add more on top. If the service was poor, speak with a manager first, then consider a lower percentage instead of none at all, since many servers depend on tips for most of their income.

        Split the Bill: Tip per Person

        A shared check is where a bill splitter earns its keep. The calculator divides the subtotal, tax, tip and total by your party size, so you see both the tip per person and the total per person. This even split works well when everyone ordered something similar and wants to split the bill without arguments.

        When one guest had several drinks and another had only a salad, an itemized split is fairer. In that case, add up each person's items, apply the same tax rate and tip percentage to their own subtotal, and then total it all. Anyone who wants a quick bill split for a large group can do an even division first, then adjust for the exceptions. To split the check well, agree on the tip percentage before anyone pays, so nobody feels the gratuity was chosen for them.

        Tip base
        The part of the bill your percentage multiplies, either the subtotal or the subtotal plus tax.
        Gratuity
        Another word for a tip, usually printed on a receipt when it has been added automatically.
        Service charge
        A fee the restaurant adds for large parties or events, which may or may not go to the staff.

        Tipping Customs Around the World

        Tipping is not universal, and what is polite in one country can be awkward in another. In the United States and Canada it is widely expected at restaurants, while in Japan tips are often refused and can be seen as insulting. Elsewhere in Europe, a service charge is frequently included, and diners round up the bill or leave the change as a modest thank-you. Tipping rules differ across countries, and because tipping norms are tied to local culture, the right amount of money in one place can be wrong in the next.

        • United States and Canada: 15% to 20% is expected at sit-down restaurants.
        • Brazil and Chile: around 10%, often already included as a service line.
        • Australia and China: not expected, though a small gesture for a tour guide is welcome.
        • Japan: no tipping, and good service is simply part of the price.

        When you travel, research the customs of each country before you go, since tipping in some countries is expected and in others discouraged. It is also worth paying in the local currency, because converting a tip at a poor exchange rate quietly shrinks the money your server receives. In many places, handing over cash rather than adding the tip to a card payment is the most direct way to make sure the gratuity reaches the person who earned it, and leaving cash is often the more welcome option. Once you know the local norm, enter that percentage into the calculator to see the tip amount for your bill.

        How Service Quality Sets the Tip Amount You Enter

        A fixed habit is easy, but a sliding scale respects what actually happened at the table. The percentage is the one input the calculator cannot guess for you: it multiplies your base by whatever you type, and it tells the server how the visit went.

        • 10%: the service had real problems, such as long unexplained waits or wrong orders that were never fixed.
        • 15%: acceptable service, with nothing memorable either way.
        • 18% to 20%: attentive, friendly service where drinks were refilled and mistakes were handled quickly.
        • 25% or more: exceptional service, a complicated order handled gracefully, or a table that stayed long after the kitchen closed.

        Tipping on discounts and coupons

        If you pay with a coupon or a happy-hour discount, tip on the amount the meal would have cost before the discount. Your server carried the same plates either way, and a smaller bill should not shrink their pay. For example, a $72.00 order reduced to $60.00 by a coupon still deserves 18% of the original figure, which is $12.96, and not 18% of the reduced one. In the calculator, enter the pre-discount amount in the bill field.

        Common mistakes to avoid

        The most frequent error is tipping twice: once through an automatic service charge and again by hand, so check the bill field before adding anything. The second is entering the post-tax total in the bill field when you meant to use the subtotal, which nudges every result upward. The third is forgetting to round per-person shares consistently, so the table ends up a few cents short. Reading the receipt carefully before you open the calculator prevents all three.

        Tip Calculator questions

        How much should I tip at a restaurant?

        In the United States, 15% to 20% of the bill is the usual range for good service at a sit-down restaurant, with 18% to 20% common in large cities. Counter service or coffee often warrants $1 to $2 or about 10%.

        Should I tip on the pre-tax or post-tax amount?

        Both are common. Many diners tip on the pre-tax subtotal because sales tax is not part of the service. Tick "Do not tip on tax" and enter the tax to calculate the tip on the pre-tax amount.

        How do I calculate a tip without a calculator?

        Move the decimal point one place left to find 10% of the bill, double it for 20%, or add half of the 10% figure to get 15%.

        How do I split the bill and tip evenly?

        Enter the number of people. The calculator divides the tip and the total evenly and shows the tip per person and total per person.

        What if a service charge is already on my bill?

        Check the receipt first. If an automatic gratuity is included, you generally do not need to add another tip, though you can add extra for exceptional service.

        What is the formula for a tip?

        Tip = tip base x tip percentage / 100, and total = bill + tip. To get the final amount in one step, multiply the bill by 1 plus the decimal tip rate, for example 1.18 for an 18% tip.

        Is tipping expected in every country?

        No. Tipping is widely expected in the United States and Canada, but in countries such as Japan it is often declined or considered rude, and in much of Europe a service charge is already included.
