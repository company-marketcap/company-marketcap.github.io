        What Is a Percentage and Why Does It Matter?

        A percentage is a way of writing a number as a fraction of 100. The word comes from the Latin per centum, meaning "by the hundred," and the same root appears in cent and century. Because every percent is out of 100, percentages give you relative amounts you can compare even when the underlying totals are different sizes. Try the free basic calculator to run your own numbers — everything is calculated in your browser and nothing you enter is stored or sent anywhere.

        What is a percentage in everyday life?

        You meet percentages in statistics, interest rates, nutrition panels, stock prices and sale signs. A percentage is a dimensionless ratio, so 40% means the same thing whether you are talking about 40 of 100 students or 400 of 1,000 customers. That is why mathematics teachers, finance teams and shoppers all lean on the same idea.

        Percentage of a whole and the percent sign

        When you want the percentage of a whole, the part is compared with the total, and the result is written with the percent sign (%). The whole is always 100%. A part that is smaller than the whole gives a percentage below 100, and a part larger than the whole gives an improper percentage above 100, such as 140%.

        Percentage Formula: The Math Behind Every Result

        Every percentage calculation is one algebraic equation with three values: the part, the whole and the rate. Rearranging it lets you solve for whichever one is missing. The basic percentage formula is:

        $$\text{Percentage} = \frac{\text{Part}}{\text{Whole}} \times 100$$

        To find the part when you know the rate, multiply instead:

        $$\text{Part} = \text{Whole} \times \frac{P}{100}$$

        And to find the whole from a known part and rate, divide:

        $$\text{Whole} = \frac{\text{Part}}{P \div 100}$$

        Notice that the percentage formula always involves a division or a multiplication by 100. That step is simply a switch between the decimal form and the percent form, which the calculator performs for you automatically.

        How to calculate percentages by hand

        If you want to know how to calculate percentages without a tool, follow the same three moves: divide the part by the total, multiply by 100, and attach the percent sign. For the percentage of a number, reverse the process: convert the rate to a decimal, then multiply it by the number.

        Worked Example: Finding a Percentage of a Number

        Suppose a customer owes a bill of $1,240 and a service charge of 7.5% applies. You want the percentage of a number, so convert 7.5% to the decimal 0.075 and multiply: 0.075 × 1,240 = 93. The service charge is $93.00, and the bill grows to $1,333.

        Percent to Decimal and Decimal to Percent Conversions

        Converting is the quickest skill to master. To go from percent to decimal, divide by 100, which moves the decimal point two places to the left: 7.5% becomes 0.075. To go from decimal to percent, multiply by 100 and move the point two places to the right: 0.0625 becomes 6.25%, and 1.4 becomes 140%.

        • Percent to decimal: 62% → 0.62
        • Decimal to percent: 0.18 → 18%
        • Fraction to percent: 3/8 → 37.5%
        • Hundredths: 5 hundredths is 0.05, which is 5%

        Each of these multiply or divide steps works because a percentage is a fraction of 100, so a value expressed in hundredths maps directly onto the percent sign.

        Percentage Change Calculator: Increase and Decrease

        A percentage change calculator compares a new figure with the initial value. It uses the absolute value of the gap, divided by what you started with:

        $$\text{Change} = \frac{\text{New} - \text{Initial}}{\text{Initial}} \times 100$$

        A positive answer is a percentage increase and a negative answer is a percentage decrease. Take a subscription that rises from 160 to 188: the gap is 28, and 28 ÷ 160 = 0.175, so the percentage increase is 17.5%. Run it backward from 188 to 160 and the percentage decrease is 28 ÷ 188, or about 14.9%. The two numbers differ because each change is measured against a different starting point.

        Percentage increase calculator and percentage decrease calculator uses

        People reach for a percentage increase calculator when tracking trends such as rent, wages or stock prices, and for a percentage decrease calculator when they measure a sale or a drop in costs. If you only need to add or subtract a rate, multiply the original by 1.075 for a 7.5% rise or by 0.925 for a 7.5% fall: 1,240 becomes 1,333 or 1,147.

        Percentage Difference Calculator for Two Values

        Sometimes neither figure is the "original." A percentage difference calculator treats both numbers as equals and divides their gap by their average:

        $$\text{Difference} = \frac{|V_1 - V_2|}{(V_1 + V_2)/2} \times 100$$

        Comparing 54 with 78, the gap is 24 and the average is 66, so the percentage difference is 24 ÷ 66 × 100, or about 36.4%. Use this when you compare two suppliers or two test results rather than before-and-after readings.

        A Tenant Checks a Rent Renewal: A Percentage Walkthrough

        Dana opens a renewal letter on a Tuesday evening. The rent is moving from $1,462 to $1,538 a month, and the lease Dana signed two years ago says increases may not exceed 5% per renewal. The letter calls the new figure "modest," but Dana wants the actual percentage.

        Dana picks the percentage change option, enters 1,462 as the starting value and 1,538 as the new value, and presses calculate. The gap is $76, and 76 ÷ 1,462 = 0.0520, so the calculator reports a 5.2% increase. That is a percentage change of 0.2 points above the lease's 5% ceiling, which is small but real.

        The result matters because the cap, not the landlord's wording, is the reference point. Dana runs the check in the other direction: 5% of 1,462 is $73.10, so the highest renewal the clause allows is $1,462 + $73.10 = $1,535.10. The proposed $1,538 is $2.90 over that line.

        Common Percentage Problems in Money and School

        These common percentage problems show up constantly in personal finance, business management and the classroom.

        • Discount and sale price: shopping discounts take a rate off the price, so a 30% sale leaves 70% to pay.
        • Tip and tax: a tip or tax is a percentage of the bill added on top.
        • Interest: interest is a percentage of a balance charged over a period.
        • Exam scores: a mark of 46 out of 60 is 76.7%.

        Profit percentage from cost price and selling price

        The profit percentage uses the cost price as the base: subtract the cost price from the selling price to get the profit, divide by the cost price, then multiply by 100. A seller who buys at 80 and sells at 92 earns 12 in profit, a margin of 15% on cost. That margin is simply a percentage change of the sale figure over its cost, so the percentage change formula applies, and economic indicators such as inflation and growth use that same change calculation.

        Average percentage across several results

        To find an average percentage, add the individual percentages, then divide by how many there are. If three tests score 62%, 70% and 81%, the total is 213 and the average is 71%. Keep in mind this simple method assumes every test carries equal weight.

        Reading Percentages on Food Labels and Reports

        Percentages carry real information beyond the classroom, and each one is a part divided by a whole. Food labels list a daily value for each nutrient, so a serving with 9 mg of a nutrient against a daily need of 45 mg gives 9 ÷ 45 × 100 = 20% of that day's target. The same math applies to a quarterly report: a department that spent $18,400 of a $23,000 budget has used 18,400 ÷ 23,000 × 100 = 80% of its allowance. In both cases the second figure is the denominator, and the first is the amount you compare against it.

        Pair each percentage with the size of the number behind it. A 50% change from 2 to 3 is tiny in absolute terms, because (3 − 2) ÷ 2 × 100 is the same percentage change formula that a 50% jump from 2,000 to 3,000 uses, while a 5% rise on a very large value can be huge. After any percentage calculation, quote the percentage alongside the raw value so the reader sees both the proportion and the scale.

        Tips for Accurate Percentage Calculations

        A few habits protect your accuracy. Always confirm which figure is the whole before you divide. Keep at least two decimal places until the final step, then round once. Remember that percentage points and percent are different: a rise from 4% to 6% is two percentage points but a 50% relative increase. Finally, sanity-check the answer, since a part smaller than the whole should never return more than 100%.

        Visual checks for your percentage results

        If the answer feels wrong, a shaded bar or a find percentage calculator can confirm it at a glance. Students often use that picture to build intuition for fractions, ratios and the mathematics behind them.

        Percentage Calculator questions

        What is a percentage?

        A percentage is a number expressed as a fraction of 100, written with the % sign. 25% means 25 out of every 100, or one quarter of the whole.

        How do you calculate a percentage of a number?

        Convert the percentage to a decimal by dividing by 100, then multiply it by the number. For example, 7.5% of 1,240 is 0.075 × 1,240 = 93.

        How do you find what percent one number is of another?

        Divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100. If 87 is the part and 232 is the whole, 87 ÷ 232 × 100 = 37.5%.

        How do you calculate percentage change?

        Subtract the starting value from the new value, divide by the starting value, and multiply by 100. A positive answer is an increase and a negative answer is a decrease.

        What is the difference between percentage change and percentage difference?

        Percentage change compares a new value with an original one, so order matters. Percentage difference divides the gap by the average of both values, so neither value is treated as the original.

        How do I convert a percent to a decimal?

        Divide by 100, which moves the decimal point two places left: 62% becomes 0.62. To go back, multiply by 100.

        Can a percentage be greater than 100%?

        Yes. A percentage above 100% means the part is larger than the whole it is compared with, such as a price that has more than doubled.
