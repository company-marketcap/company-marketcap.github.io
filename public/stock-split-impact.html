        How to Use the Stock Split Calculator

        Three entries drive every result, and each one is printed on your latest brokerage statement or in the company's announcement. Nothing needs to be looked up beyond that, which is why a split calculator is one of the quickest checks an investor can run.         Enter the number of shares, share price and split ratio

        • Number of shares: the whole and fractional shares you hold before the split.
        • Share price: the last closing price before the split takes effect, in dollars.
        • Split ratio: written as new shares for old, such as 4-for-1 for a forward split or 1-for-10 for a reverse split.

        Read your new share count and total value

        The result panel returns the adjusted price and the total value, with your original figures alongside. If the two totals differ, an entry is wrong, because a split only changes how your position is sliced, not what they are worth on the day. Keep the figure the broker shows next to the calculator's answer and compare them line by line.

        Calculate a Stock Split With the Split Ratio Formula

        A ratio written N-for-M means every M shares you hold become N shares. Divide N by M to get the split multiplier, then apply it in opposite directions to the share total and to the price. The same rule covers an uneven split such as 5-for-2 and a reverse split such as 1-for-10, because the multiplier is simply below 1 for these.         $$S_1 = S_0 \times \frac{N}{M}$$

        $$P_1 = P_0 \div \frac{N}{M}$$

        Here \(S_0\) and \(P_0\) are your shares and price before the split, and \(S_1\) and \(P_1\) are the same two figures after it. Multiply them back together and you land on the same total, since \(S_1 \times P_1 = S_0 \times P_0\). That identity is the whole idea behind calculating a stock split by hand and checking it against a calculator.

        Find the split-adjusted share price

        It is the old quote divided by the multiplier. Charting platforms use it to rewrite historical quotes so a drop caused only by the split does not look like a crash. When you compare a chart before and after a split, always read the split-adjusted share price rather than the raw print from that day.

        2-for-1 and other forward ratios

        A two-for-one split doubles your shares and halves the price, a 3-for-1 triples and thirds them, and a 5-for-2 grows them by two and a half times. Large technology names have used 20-for-1 ratios when quotes climbed far beyond what retail investors wanted to pay for a single share.         Stock Split Calculation Example: Shares Owned and Share Price Before and After

        Say you own 135 shares of a company quoted at $412.80, and the board announces a 6-for-1 split. Your position is worth 135 × $412.80 = $55,728.00 before anything happens.

        1. Work out the multiplier: 6 ÷ 1 = 6.
        2. Multiply your shares: 135 × 6 = 810 shares.
        3. Divide the price: $412.80 ÷ 6 = $68.80 per share.
        4. Check the total: 810 × $68.80 = $55,728.00, exactly where you started.

        Pre-split and post-split holdings side by side

        Nothing was created or destroyed. You hold six times as many pieces, each worth a sixth as much, so a stock split calculation always ends with matching totals. What changes is the lot size you can trade: selling one share before the split meant raising $412.80, afterwards it means $68.80.

        Reverse Stock Split Calculator: Fewer Shares at a Higher Price

        A reverse stock split calculator runs the same formula with a multiplier below one. In a 1-for-8 reverse stock split every eight shares merge into one, and the share price is multiplied by eight.

        Take 2,405 shares priced at $0.85, worth $2,044.25. Divide the shares by eight and you get 300.625, so you receive 300 whole shares at $6.80 each, which is $2,040.00. The leftover 0.625 share is usually paid out in cash, worth 0.625 × $6.80 = $4.25. The two figures add up to $2,044.25, so a calculator that reports both pieces matches your statement to the cent.

        Why reverse stock splits happen

        Companies use reverse stock splits to lift a low quote back above the minimum price that a stock exchange's listing requirements demand. A listing on the NASDAQ or NYSE can be at risk when shares trade under $1 for too long, and a reverse stock split is the fastest way to fix that. Some institutional investors also avoid low-priced names, so a higher quote widens who can buy. It can still signal trouble, so read the filing before drawing a conclusion. A forward split is the mirror image, and a forward stock split is nearly always announced after a period of growth.

        Fractional shares and the new share price

        Brokerages differ on leftovers: some pay cash for the fraction, some round up or down, and a few hold fractional positions. Ask which rule applies before the effective date. The new share price is unaffected either way; only the cash in lieu changes.

        Checking a 15-for-1 Stock Split Before the Effective Date

        A press release lands on a Tuesday: the company will split 15-for-1, effective after the close on the 19th. Priya holds 62 lots of it, bought over several years, and the last close is $1,290.60. Before the date arrives she wants to know what her account should show and what to do with an open order.

        She types 62 into the first field, $1,290.60 into the price field and 15 for 1 into the ratio, then clicks the calculator's button. The answer comes back as 930 shares at $86.04, and the before-and-after totals both read $80,017.20. That matches her statement, which shows 62 × $1,290.60, so the entries are right.

        Next she checks the figure against something outside the calculator: the company's Form 8-K, which states one share for every share held plus 14 more. Fifteen in all for each one held fits the 15-for-1 ratio, so her 930 is what she should see on the payable date.

Dividing by 15 gives $91.67, so she cancels the order and re-enters it at that figure on the morning of the 20th, after confirming her broker shows 930 shares. The multiplication is trivial; having the new numbers in hand before the open is what lets her act before the price print on the 20th rather than reacting to it.

        Why Companies Announce Stock Splits

        A stock split is a corporate action approved by the board of directors, and it is mostly about perception and access. When a quote reaches four figures, a single share can swallow a small account, which blocks retail investors from diversifying. Cutting the price makes each share more affordable and more accessible, widening the investor base without touching the business itself.

        Liquidity and trading

        Lower quotes tend to bring tighter trading and more buyers and sellers, which means more liquidity. That helps existing shareholders sell without moving the quote. It does not change fundamentals, and splits are not dilutive: no new money enters the company and your ownership percentage stays put.

        Does market capitalization change?

        No. Market capitalization remains unchanged because outstanding shares rise by the same factor that the quote falls. Equity value and the intrinsic value of the business are the same either side of the split, so a split is neither a bonus nor a loss. Any move you see in the following weeks comes from investor sentiment, not arithmetic.

        Stock Split Effects on Cost Basis, Dividends and Taxes

        Your per-share cost basis is divided by the multiplier when your shares multiply and multiplied on a reverse split, while the total you paid stays fixed. If you bought at $318.60 and the company declares a 6-for-1 stock split, each share is carried at $53.10 afterwards, and 810 shares still add up to $43,011.00. Most brokerages adjust this automatically, though you should compare their figure with yours before tax reporting season, since the IRS taxes gains per share.

        Dividends are rescaled the same way. A dividend of a given amount per share before the split becomes a smaller figure afterwards, so your total payout stays level. Check the declared dividends per share on the next payment date and expect it to match the split ratio.

        What to do before the split date

        • Run your holdings through the calculator and save the result for your records.
        • Check how your broker handles fractional shares and rounding.
        • Update any limit orders and price alerts, which may be cancelled or left at the old price.
        • Record your new cost per share for later gains and losses.

        Keep in mind that this calculator shows the mechanics only. It does not predict where the market will take the quote afterwards, and it is not investment advice. A split changes the shape of your position, not your risk, so make the real decision on the company's growth and valuation rather than on the headline ratio.

        Stock Split Planning for Your Portfolio

        A stock split changes the number of pieces, not the weight of a position, so your portfolio allocation is identical the moment the market reopens. If a name was 6% of the account at the close, it is still 6% at the open. The calculator shows this directly: its two totals match to the cent, so the position's weight in your account cannot move. What does shift is the lot size, because the lower quote it returns lets you trim or add in smaller steps.

        Why the price change on split day is not a loss

        On the effective date a 6-for-1 stock split shows a price change of about -83.3% on an unadjusted chart, because $68.80 sits 83.3% below $412.80. Nothing was lost; the quote simply reflects the multiplier. A shareholder who sees red on that day should compare the move with the ratio before reacting, and look at the adjusted history rather than the raw print.

        Stock splits are not a signal on their own

        Plenty of financial writers treat a forward stock split as good news and a reverse split as bad news, but the ratio itself carries no cash. Treat it as context for your investment strategy, not a trigger. If several of your stocks announce a stock split in the same quarter, run each one through the calculator separately, because every forward ratio and every reverse split ratio needs its own entries. Your existing shares convert automatically, and the capital you had in the position stays invested at the same market value, so the only real task is updating your records and, where relevant, your plan for investing new money.

        Stock Split Calculator questions

        What is a stock split?

        A stock split is a corporate action in which a company divides each existing share into several new ones. The share price falls by the same factor, so the total value of your holding is unchanged immediately afterwards.

        How do I calculate the share price after a stock split?

        Divide the pre-split share price by the split multiplier, which is the new shares in the ratio divided by the old shares. For a 6-for-1 split the multiplier is 6, so a $412.80 share becomes $68.80.

        How do I calculate a reverse stock split?

        Select Reverse Split and enter the ratio, for example 1 new share for every 10 old shares. Your share count is multiplied by 0.1 and the price by 10, so the total value stays the same.

        Does a stock split change the value of my investment?

        No. Your total value is the same immediately before and after a split, because you hold more or fewer shares at a proportionally lower or higher price. Later moves in the price come from the market, not from the split.

        What happens to my cost basis after a stock split?

        Your total cost basis does not change, but the cost per share does. Divide your original cost per share by the split multiplier for a forward split, or multiply it for a reverse split, and update your records for tax reporting.

        How do stock splits affect dividends?

        The dividend per share is adjusted in proportion to the split, so the total dividend you receive stays about the same. After a 2-for-1 split, for example, the per-share payment halves while you hold twice as many shares.

        Why do companies split their stock?

        Forward splits lower the price per share, which makes shares more affordable for retail investors and can improve liquidity. Reverse splits raise a low price, often to meet a stock exchange's minimum listing price.

        Can a reverse stock split be a bad sign?

        Sometimes. Reverse splits are more common among struggling companies, but they can also be a routine way to regain exchange compliance, so check the company's filing and wider financial situation before drawing a conclusion.
