        How the APR Mortgage Calculator Works

        Unlike a general APR calculator that stops at a fee total, this tool starts with the monthly payment your interest rate produces, then asks a different question: if the money you really receive is smaller than the loan amount because fees were deducted at closing, what yearly rate makes those same payments repay it? That answer is the APR. Because the payment stays the same while the usable cash shrinks, the APR is always higher than the note rate whenever you pay any upfront fees. If you want to see how the figures change, the existing mortgage biweekly calculator online gives you an instant result you can adjust as you go.

        What You Enter

        The inputs mirror what appears on a lender's quote. You supply four things, and the calculator does the rest:

        • Loan amount: the home price minus your down payment.
        • Interest rate: the stated yearly rate on the note, before any fees.
        • Loan term: the number of years you will repay, most often 15, 20 or 30.
        • Upfront fees: every finance charge you pay at closing, including points.

        Which Fees Belong in the Calculation

        Under the Truth in Lending Act, a lender must disclose an APR so that every borrower can compare offers. Not every closing charge counts toward it, so ask each lender which items it folded in. Charges that usually raise the APR are listed below, along with those that normally stay outside it.

        Mortgage APR Calculator Formula and Worked Example

        Two steps produce the answer. First, the monthly mortgage payment of principal and interest, with monthly rate \(i\) and \(n\) payments: Next, open the 20 vs 30 year mortgage calculator and enter your own details to see an estimate in seconds.

        $$M = P \times \frac{i}{1-(1+i)^{-n}}$$

        Second, the APR is the monthly rate \(i_a\) that makes those payments worth only the amount financed, which is the loan minus fees \(F\), and then the result is multiplied by twelve:

        $$P - F = M \times \frac{1-(1+i_a)^{-n}}{i_a}, \qquad \text{APR} = 12 \times i_a$$

        There is no tidy closed form for \(i_a\), so the calculator searches for it numerically, which is why a tool is far easier than a pencil.

        Our Sample Home Purchase

        Say you buy a home for $465,000 and make a down payment of $69,750 (15%). That leaves a loan amount of $395,250 at a 6.25% interest rate over a 30-year term. The lender charges the upfront fees below, all paid in cash at closing:

Since only $387,468.50 of cash actually reaches the seller after fees, the calculator reports a real APR of 6.439%, which is 0.189 percentage points above the note rate.

        Reading Your Mortgage Loan APR Result

        The gap between the note rate and the APR is the cost of the fees, expressed as a yearly rate. A small gap means the lender charges little beyond interest. A wide gap deserves a closer look at the fee list. As a rule of thumb, each additional $4,000 or so of fees on this sample loan adds roughly 0.1 percentage point to the APR, so you can see quickly how a fee change shifts the total cost.

        Annual Percentage Rate vs Interest Rate vs APY

        People mix up these three figures constantly, and a lender's marketing rarely helps. The interest rate prices only the borrowed principal and sets your monthly payment. Your mortgage loan annual percentage rate folds in fees. The annual percentage yield (APY), also called the effective annual rate, adds the effect of compound interest and is what savings accounts and other deposit accounts advertise.

        Why APR Looks Smaller Than APY

        A mortgage APR is a monthly-period rate multiplied by twelve, so it ignores intra-year compounding. Converting your 6.25% rate to its yearly equivalent gives \((1 + 0.0625/12)^{12} - 1 \approx 6.43\%\). That is why a bank prefers to show APR on a loan, where the number looks lower, and APY on a savings account, where it looks higher. When you compare loans, compare APR with APR so the basis stays the same.

        Fixed Rate and Variable Rate Loans

        With a fixed-rate mortgage, the interest rate and therefore the payment stay constant, so the APR is a firm number. A variable rate works differently. An adjustable-rate mortgage starts low and then resets according to an index such as the federal funds rate or a market benchmark, with a credit-based margin added on top of it by the lender. This calculator computes a fixed-rate APR, so for an adjustable loan a variable APR built on the starting rate is only an estimate, not a promise. The longer the loan term, the more a rate swing can change what you really pay.

        Comparing Two Lenders with the Mortgage APR Calculator

        Marguerite Osei has a signed contract on a townhouse and two Loan Estimates on her desk, both for a $287,400 mortgage over 30 years. Lender X quotes 6.75% with $5,236 in upfront charges. Lender Y quotes 6.50%, but its charges total $9,118, because one discount point alone costs $2,874.

        She enters X's figures first: loan amount 287,400, rate 6.75, term 30 years, fees 5,236. The calculator returns a monthly payment of $1,864.07 and an APR of 6.930%. Then she enters Y's figures: the payment drops to $1,816.56, while the APR reads 6.813%. Even with $3,882 more due at closing, Y's mortgage APR is 0.117 percentage points lower.

        The APR alone does not settle it, because it assumes she keeps the loan for 30 years. So she divides the extra $3,882 by the $47.51 she saves each month and gets about 82 months, or roughly 6.8 years, to recover the added cost. Her employer's relocation window runs at least ten years, which clears that break-even point by more than three years. She asks Lender Y to confirm the figures in writing on a fresh Loan Estimate and locks the 6.50% offer, planning to rerun the numbers with a 7-year horizon only if the move date changes.

        When the Total Annual Cost Is Misleading

        APR spreads upfront fees across the entire loan term, which assumes you keep the mortgage until the final payment. Most people do not. A sale, a refinance or extra payments will end the loan early, and when that happens the fees look more expensive than the APR suggested because they are recovered over fewer years.

        Discount Points and Your Break-Even Time

        Take a second offer on the same home: a 5.875% rate with two points. Its upfront charges rise to $11,734.00, the monthly payment falls to $2,338.05, and the 30-year APR drops to 6.156%, so on paper it wins. Test the shorter horizon too. If you plan to sell and pay off the loan after 7 years, the APR for the first offer climbs to 6.618% and the second to 6.427%, so the lower rate still comes out ahead, but by a smaller margin. Keep paying points only if you expect to hold the loan past the time it takes to earn the extra cost back.

        • Offer one: 6.25% with one point and a $2,433.62 payment.
        • Offer two: 5.875% with two points and a $2,338.05 payment.

        Costs the APR Does Not Capture

        The APR this calculator returns covers only finance charges, so it is not a full budget. It omits property taxes, homeowners insurance and any HOA dues, none of which are part of principal and interest. If your down payment is under 20%, private mortgage insurance (PMI) is added to what you owe monthly, and you can usually request its removal once your equity reaches 20%. Price these separately before you decide what you can afford.

        How to Calculate Your Mortgage APR and Compare Lenders

        The fastest route is to gather the same figures from every lender, calculate each APR the same way, and compare the results in one list. A lender's Loan Estimate is the document to use, since every bank must provide it in the same layout.

        Steps to Compare Two Mortgage Offers

        • Request a Loan Estimate from each lender on the same day and for the same loan amount.
        • Copy the interest rate, the loan term and every item in the origination and services sections.
        • Add up upfront fees, including discount points and any mortgage broker fees.
        • Run each offer through the calculator and note the real APR next to the monthly payment.
        • Check the total cost over the time you expect to keep the loan, not only the 30-year figure.

        Loan Types That Change the Math

        The loan type you choose affects both fees and APR. A conventional loan with a small down payment adds mortgage insurance. FHA loans carry their own insurance premiums, while VA loans charge a funding fee and USDA loans charge a guarantee fee, and each of these sits inside the APR for that loan type. A first-time homebuyer who qualifies for down payment help should recalculate, because a smaller balance lowers both the payment and the fees.

        Ways to Lower Your Mortgage Loan APR

        You have more control over your APR than over market mortgage rates. A few moves reliably trim the figure, and the calculator shows the effect of each before you commit:

        • Negotiate lender charges: origination and processing fees are often adjustable, and asking one lender to match another's quote can remove hundreds.
        • Improve your credit: a higher credit score can reduce both the rate and the fees, which compounds into a lower APR.
        • Raise your down payment: a bigger share paid in cash shrinks the loan and may eliminate PMI.
        • Choose a shorter loan term: a 15-year loan usually carries a lower note rate, and therefore a lower APR, while spreading the same fees over fewer years.
        • Buy points only with a long horizon: discount points pay off over time, not right away.

        Refinancing, Prepaying and the APR

        If you are refinancing, the refinance fees count as upfront charges, so run the numbers for the new loan the same way. Once you own the home, extra payments build your home equity faster, and you can prepay principal with extra payments, which cuts interest and shortens the payoff date, though it does not change the APR already disclosed on the original loan. A mortgage insurance removal request or a drop in mortgage rates are both reasons to run the calculation again.

        Fees That Move the Total Annual Cost of Your Loan

        Several charges are easy to miss on the first read of a quote, and each one either belongs in the calculator's upfront fees field or does not. Enter administration fees, application fees and any escrow fees a lender calls a finance charge, because they raise the APR. Leave out appraisal fees, survey fees and title insurance when the quote lists them as third-party costs, since they usually sit outside the disclosed figure. Treat any out-of-pocket fees you pay at closing as upfront charges, and ask each lender for an itemized list so the same cost is not counted twice, or missed, when you compare.

        Rerun your calculation on every fresh Loan Estimate before you lock, because quotes shift with the market and the Federal Reserve's moves can change them within days. Pair the result with your closing costs, escrow deposits and expected holding period, and an amortization schedule (also called an amortization table) will show how each payment splits between principal and your origination points and interest. Higher inflation is one reason lenders reprice, which is why a fresh APR beats an old one.

        APR Mortgage Calculator questions

        What is a mortgage APR?

        The mortgage APR, or annual percentage rate, is the yearly cost of your loan once interest and certain upfront finance charges are combined. It lets you compare offers that have different rates and fees on the same basis.

        Why is my APR higher than my interest rate?

        Fees and points reduce the cash you actually receive while your payment stays the same. The rate that makes those payments repay the smaller amount is higher than the note rate, so the APR sits above it whenever you pay upfront charges.

        Which fees go into the APR?

        Points, origination, application, underwriting, processing and mortgage broker fees, plus mortgage insurance, normally count. Appraisal, survey and title charges, prepaid taxes and escrow deposits are usually left out, so ask each lender for an itemized list.

        Does a lower APR always mean a cheaper loan?

        Not always. APR spreads upfront fees over the full loan term, so if you sell or refinance early those fees cost more per year than the APR suggests. Compare the monthly payment and your expected holding period as well.

        How do discount points change the APR?

        Each point costs 1% of the loan and usually lowers the interest rate. The points raise your upfront fees, so enter them in the Points field and compare the resulting APR with a no-points offer.

        What is the difference between APR and APY?

        APR for a loan uses a monthly period and ignores compounding within the year, while APY includes compounding and is mainly quoted for savings and deposit accounts. At the same rate the APY is higher than the APR.

        How does this calculator treat PMI?

        The yearly PMI amount you enter is added to each monthly payment while your balance is above 80% of the house value, and those payments are included in the APR calculation.

        Is the APR the same for adjustable-rate mortgages?

        This calculator assumes a fixed rate for the whole term. For an adjustable-rate mortgage the result is an estimate based on the starting rate, because later rate changes are unknown.
